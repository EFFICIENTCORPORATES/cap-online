## Scene 1 — Rescue before winding up
- **Open:** "A company is like a person: it can be healthy, ill, or finished."
- **Points:**
  - Buyback is the *happy* company: too much cash, gives it back to shareholders. Real cash goes **out**. Normal business.
  - Foundation taught you winding-up (the process) and dissolution (the outcome). That is the **end**.
  - But a sick company should not be buried at the first sign of illness. **Try to save it first.**
  - Rescue 1: fix it from inside (internal reconstruction). Rescue 2: move the business into a new body (external reconstruction). Only if both fail, winding up.
  - And a healthy company can do the opposite: **grow** (amalgamate, absorb, buy control).
- **Ask:** "A company loses money for five years. Is the first thing to do to close it?"
- **Trap:** students think reconstruction = closing the company. Say it clearly: **internal reconstruction: same company continues.**
- **Bridge:** "Let us look at the first rescue."

## Scene 2 — Internal reconstruction
- **Open:** "The balance sheet is lying: it shows assets that are not worth what they say, and a big loss hanging on the left."
- **Points:**
  - Overvalued assets plus accumulated losses = a balance sheet that does not show the truth.
  - Who can help? Shareholders accept a cut, debenture holders waive some claim or interest, creditors accept less. Nobody is paid cash; they **sacrifice**.
  - All the sacrifices go into **one account** (Capital Reduction / Reconstruction Account). That account is then used to wipe the loss and the overvalued assets.
  - The name gets "and Reduced" added.
- **Mention, do not explain:** alteration of capital, variation of rights, reduction, compromise, surrender (Ch 14 topic 2).
- **Ask:** "Does any cash leave the company here?" (No.)
- **Bridge:** "Now the big question for the rest of the block: **does the other company die or survive?**"

## Scene 3 — The big fork
- **Open:** "Everything else in these chapters comes from one question: when two companies come together, does one of them disappear?"
- **Points:**
  - **Dies:** reconstruction by new company, amalgamation, absorption. One-time event. One date. Everything settled that day. **AS 14.**
  - **Lives:** subsidiary, associate, joint venture. An ongoing relationship. The target keeps trading and keeps earning. **AS 21, 23, 27.**
  - Read the contrast table slowly. **Pre/post split: no / yes. Minority: no / yes.**
- **Ask:** "If the company is wound up tomorrow, can it declare a dividend next year?" (No: so no pre/post.)
- **Trap:** mixing up "acquisition" (target lives) with "amalgamation" (target dies). Say: "**Acquisition = the target survives.**"
- **Bridge:** "Let us take the dying side first."

## Scene 4 — Target dies
- **Open:** "There are three ways a company can die into another."
- **Points (Layer A: what happens):**
  - **External reconstruction:** a new company takes over the business of an existing sick company, and it is usually the same set of shareholders. Compare with internal: there, the old company stays.
  - **Amalgamation (A + B -> C):** two companies close and a new one is born. Marriage where both leave their parents' homes and start a new home.
  - **Absorption (A absorbs B):** one company swallows another; the big one continues.
- **Points (Layer B: how we account):**
  - "Feelings do not decide the accounting. **Five conditions decide.**" Read them: all assets and liabilities, 90 percent shareholders, consideration wholly in equity shares, business continues, no revaluation.
  - **All five pass = merger = pooling of interests. Any one fails = purchase.**
  - Two traps to say aloud: an absorption of your own subsidiary at book values can be a *merger*; an A + B -> C where the new company revalues assets is a *purchase*.
  - ICAI itself notes external reconstruction is essentially an amalgamation in the nature of merger (Ch 14, p14.3).
  - **Words:** AS 14 says "consideration for the amalgamation"; in a purchase we usually say "purchase consideration".
- **Table to read aloud:** merger = shares only, share swap, all reserves carried; purchase = any form of payment, buyout, only statutory reserves carried.
- **Mention only:** Realisation A/c; the four ways to compute the price (lump sum, net payment, net assets, intrinsic value / share exchange ratio).
- **Ask:** "A swallows B but pays all shareholders in A's equity shares, keeps book values: merger or purchase?" (Merger, if all five pass.)
- **Trap:** equating the *form* (amalgamation or absorption) with the *method* (merger or purchase).
- **Bridge:** "Now the other side, where the company lives. And here the first question is: how much of it do I own?"

## Scene 5 — The ownership ladder
- **Open:** "Think of your friends. A stranger, a friend, family."
- **Points:**
  - Below 20 percent: **stranger**. You just invested. AS 13. Shown at cost.
  - 20 to 50 percent: **friend**. You can influence, not command. AS 23. Equity method.
  - Above 50 percent: **father and son**. You control. AS 21. We report **their** assets as if they were ours.
  - Joint control: **business partners**. AS 27.
  - Standalone books always show cost; the special methods show up only in the **consolidated** statements.
- **Hostile takeover animation:** "The buyer buys **shares** from shareholders. The company is still there. It just has a new boss." -> subsidiary.
- **Say clearly:** "Buying **shares** does not kill a company. Buying the **business** does."
- **Ask:** "Hostile takeover: does the target company disappear?" (No.)
- **Bridge:** "To talk about control and cost, we need one word first: net assets."

## Scene 6 — Net assets (Arjun)
- **Open:** "Arjun starts a company with ₹50,000 of his own money."
- **Points:**
  - Step 1: capital 50; he spends it on cash 5, inventory 10, investment 15, PPE 20. Total assets 50, equity 50. **Net assets = 50.**
  - Step 2: a year of business, profit 50 (this is the **net results of operations**: what the business earned). Look at the T: equity side now has capital 50 plus reserves 50; payables 20 on the left; assets 120 on the right. **Assets 120 minus liabilities 20 = 100 = equity = net assets.** "A profit of 50 raised net assets by 50."
  - Step 3: Arjun borrows ₹10 lakh and buys PPE and keeps cash. Both sides jump to 1,120. **Net assets: still 100.**
  - "So what changes net assets? Only a change in what the owners own: profit, new capital, loss. Borrowing makes the balance sheet bigger, not richer."
  - "When you buy shares, you are buying a share of **net assets**, which is a share of **equity**, which is capital plus reserves."
- **Ask:** "If Arjun borrows another ₹10 lakh, what happens to net assets?" (No change.) "If he makes a loss of 20?" (Down by 20.)
- **Trap:** adding liabilities when computing the value of the company. "Creditors are paid first; owners own what is left."
- **Bridge:** "Now suppose another company, P Ltd, wants to buy this company."

## Scene 7 — Control and what it triggers
- **Open:** "The moment P holds more than half, or controls the board, one word is switched on: **control**."
- **Points:**
  - "If I control it, I must report it." Everything the subsidiary owns and owes is under my control, so it appears in my statements: **line by line**.
  - Five things switch on together: line-by-line, cost of control, pre/post, minority interest (only if not 100 percent), intra-group adjustments. "That is why the chapter feels crowded: it is five ideas arriving at once."
  - **Line-by-line is not adding every item of two balance sheets.** P's balance sheet has one line, "Investment in S: ₹120". That one line is **already** P's share of S's net assets (plus the premium). In the consolidated statement the investment line **dissolves** and S's real assets and liabilities take its place.
  - The 130 of net assets splits: 104 belongs to P (cancels the investment), 16 is what P paid extra (goodwill), 26 belongs to outsiders (minority interest).
  - Toggle to 100 percent: no minority slice. "**That is the only difference between a wholly owned and a partly owned subsidiary.**"
- **Control definition:** AS 21 = more than half voting power or control of the board. AS 18's definition is wider (do not quote it until we have the text).
- **Mention:** exclusions (temporary control, severe long-term restrictions), syllabus limits (one subsidiary, no cross-holding, no foreign subsidiary, no disposal).
- **Ask:** "P owns 55 percent. Does it report 55 percent of S's assets or all of S's assets?" (All: it controls them; the other 45 percent shows as minority interest.)
- **Trap:** "consolidation = adding two balance sheets". It is **replacing one line**.

## Scene 8 — Cost of control
- **Open:** "Did P pay more or less than what it received?"
- **Points:**
  - What P paid: ₹120 (cost of investment). What P received: 80 percent of net assets = ₹104.
  - Paid more = **goodwill** (we paid a premium for control, reputation, future profits). Paid less = **capital reserve** (a discount, usually because the target was in trouble).
  - **Cost of investment** is the gross price in P's own books (AS 13). **Cost of control** is the *difference*, in the consolidated statements. In the consolidated statements the investment line disappears and cost of control takes its place.
  - Same idea in amalgamation (purchase method): consideration against net assets taken over.
  - Applies to **every** subsidiary, wholly or partly owned.
- **Ask:** "P pays ₹90 for the same 80 percent. What is the difference, and what is it called?" (₹14 capital reserve.)
- **Trap:** saying cost of control arises only for wholly owned subsidiaries.

## Scene 9 — Pre- and post-acquisition
- **Open:** "S Ltd was not born the day P bought it. It earned profits before. Whose are they?"
- **Points:**
  - Timeline: April to March. P buys on **1 January**: nine months before, three after. (Always nine and three, so nobody mistakes which side is which.)
  - **Before 1 January = pre-acquisition = capital.** P *paid* for it when it bought the shares: it was inside the price. **After 1 January = post-acquisition = revenue.** Earned while P owns it.
  - S earns ₹40 in the year, evenly: ₹30 before, ₹10 after.
  - The stack animation (read slowly): "At 31 March S's equity is 140: capital 50, statutory reserve 10, general reserve 15, profit and loss 65. Draw the line. Everything up to the acquisition date is **pre**: 130. Only **10** is post."
  - "Now split by who owns it: P owns 80, minority owns 20."
  - **P's pre = 104** goes into the cost-of-control calculation: it is **used up** against the investment; it never shows as a reserve. **Minority's pre = 26** goes to minority interest. **P's post = 8** joins the group's reserves. **Minority's post = 2** goes to minority interest.
  - Check: minority = 26 + 2 = 28 = 20 percent of 140. P = 104 + 8 = 112 = 80 percent of 140.
  - **Net results of operations:** the profit the subsidiary makes. Outsiders own a share of both net assets and net results of operations: that is how ICAI defines minority interest.
  - Mention: **dividends**: paid out of pre-acquisition profit = return of my investment (credit Investment A/c), out of post-acquisition = income. Profit for a part-year is split on a **time basis** unless told otherwise. Bonus shares by the subsidiary.
  - **Why this does not happen in amalgamation:** the target is gone on one day, so there is no "after".
- **Ask:** "Of S's ₹30 profit earned before 1 January, who 'owns' it today, P or S's old shareholders?" (P paid for it: it is capital to P.)
- **Trap:** crediting a pre-acquisition dividend to the P&L; treating post-acquisition profit as capital; forgetting that the pre-acquisition *current year* profit (the 30) is also capital.

## Scene 10 — Minority interest
- **Open:** "If I own 80 percent, who owns the other 20?"
- **Points:**
  - Outsiders. They own 20 percent of net assets at acquisition (26) and 20 percent of the profit since (2): total **28**.
  - It is **not** a liability to be repaid. It is the outsiders' share of the group's net assets.
  - Wholly owned subsidiary: toggle to 100 percent. The slice disappears. Nothing else changes.
  - Where it sits: its own line after shareholders' funds (details in a later lecture).
- **Ask:** "P buys 100 percent. Is there minority interest? Is there cost of control?" (No; yes.)
- **Trap:** treating MI as a loan.

## Scene 11 — Statutory reserves
- **Open:** "Statutory reserves are reserves the law says must stay as reserves. The question is when that matters."
- **Points:**
  - **Merger (pooling):** all reserves carried as they are, including statutory ones.
  - **Purchase:** the vendor's reserves are **not** carried over, **except statutory reserves**, which must keep their identity: they are brought in through the **Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve**.
  - **Consolidation:** no special lane. The statutory 10 and general 15 sit inside the pre-acquisition block; they are part of the net worth compared with the investment, or they become minority interest. They do not appear as statutory reserves in the consolidated statements.
  - Be honest: the AS 21 chapter has **no specific rule** on statutory reserves.
- **Ask:** "Which accounting method makes you worry about *statutory* reserves?" (Purchase method of amalgamation.)
- **Trap:** carrying revenue reserves under purchase method; forgetting AAR.

## Scene 12 — Intra-group adjustments
- **Open:** "Can a group make a profit by selling to itself?"
- **Points:**
  - Inside the dashed line it is one economic family. A sale from S to P at a profit has made **no** profit for the group until P sells the goods outside.
  - So unrealised profit in stock must be removed; mutual debts cancel; asset transfers adjust profit and depreciation.
  - Concept only today. The calculations arrive in the AS 21 lecture.
- **Ask:** "S sells goods to P at a profit; P still has them at year-end. Has the group earned that profit?" (No.)

## Scene 13 — Associates and joint ventures
- **Open:** "What if I own 45 percent, not 51?"
- **Points (AS 13 vs equity method):**
  - Same investment, two treatments. Use ICAI's numbers: cost ₹15,00,000.
  - **AS 13:** the investment sits at cost. B earns ₹3,00,000? Ignored. B pays a ₹1,00,000 dividend? My 45 percent = ₹45,000 is **income**.
  - **Equity method:** B earns ₹3,00,000, my 45 percent = ₹1,35,000, so the investment **grows** to ₹16,35,000. B pays dividend: my ₹45,000 is just a **cash return of part of the investment**, so the carrying amount **shrinks** to ₹15,90,000. If it ever hits zero, we stop.
  - "In the equity method, the investment is a living number: up with profits, down with dividends."
  - Read the comparison table: holding, recognition, measurement, profit, dividends.
  - **Joint ventures:** JCO (each uses own assets; book your share of revenue and expenses; no new entity), JCA (a jointly owned asset; book your share), JCE (a separate company under joint control; proportionate consolidation: your percentage of every line).
  - "Equity method = one line grows and shrinks. Proportionate = your percentage of every line. Full consolidation = every line, then give the outsiders their share."
- **Ask:** "Dividend received under the equity method: income or reduction of the investment?" (Reduction.)
- **Trap:** booking dividend as income under the equity method.
- **Examples to verify before using:** Maruti Suzuki's origin as a joint venture; Vistara merged into Air India in November 2024, say "was".

## Scene 14 — Also in this block
- **Open:** "These are the things you will meet. I only want you to know **what** they are and **where** they sit."
- Walk the table top to bottom, one line each; read the page references only after you have checked them in the PDF.
- **Cross-holding:** "In the syllabus note it is listed as **excluded**. The Companies Act (Sec 19) is the reason a subsidiary cannot ordinarily hold the shares of its own parent, with three exceptions." (Do not describe it as a normal situation.)
- Remind: foreign subsidiaries, disposal, interest at different dates are excluded from numericals at Intermediate.

## Scene 15 — Master map and word-bank
- Put the decision tree and ladder back on screen. Pin the eight words on it, one at a time, and ask students to say the plain meaning aloud:
  - **Net assets:** what the owners own (assets minus liabilities = equity).
  - **Net results of operations:** the profit the business earns.
  - **Acquisition:** buying a stake in a company that continues.
  - **Control:** more than half the votes, or the board.
  - **Cost of control:** what I paid minus my share of net assets: goodwill or capital reserve.
  - **Pre/post acquisition:** profits earned before I bought (capital) and after (revenue).
  - **Statutory reserves:** reserves the law says must stay: matter in purchase-method amalgamation.
  - **Minority interest:** the outsiders' share of net assets and profit.
- **Close:** "Next lecture: Internal reconstruction. You already know why it exists."
