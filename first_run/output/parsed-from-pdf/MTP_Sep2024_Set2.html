Anshul manufacturers purchased 20,000 Kg. of raw material at ₹170 per Kg. Direct transit cost incurred ₹5,00,000 and normal transit loss is 3%. Anshul manufacturers actually received 19,000 kg of raw material. During the year it consumed 17,600 kg of raw material.

Further information: (i) The purchase price includes ₹15 per kg as GST in respect of which full credit is allowed and will be availed by Anshul manufacturers. (ii) Assume that there is no opening stock.

Answer the following questions based on above.

MTP Sep 2024 Set 2 · Part I – Case Scenario I · Q1(a) · Marks: 2

What will be the cost of material:

  1. ₹36,00,000
  2. ₹34,00,000
  3. ₹39,00,000
  4. ₹31,00,000

Answer: (A) ₹36,00,000 — purchase price net of the recoverable GST component: (₹170−₹15) × 20,000 kg = ₹31,00,000, plus direct transit cost ₹5,00,000 = ₹36,00,000. The ₹15/kg GST is excluded from cost since full input tax credit is available (AS 2 excludes duties/taxes subsequently recoverable from the taxing authority).

AS 2 (1.5)
A common error here is including the recoverable GST component in the cost of purchase, rather than excluding it under AS 2's rule that duties/taxes subsequently recoverable from the taxing authority are never part of inventory cost.

  2. June 2022 (When the implementation work started)
  3. September 2023 (When the implementation work should have completed as per agreed terms)

A common error here is recognising the asset on the contractually agreed 15-month target date (September 2023) rather than the date it actually became ready for use (May 2024), treating a missed deadline as if it were the recognition trigger.

How should the annual maintenance and updation expenses should be accounted for:

  1. Should be capitalised with ‘Intangible Asset’
  2. Should be recognised as a separate ‘Intangible Asset’
  3. Should be recognised as expense in Profit and Loss annually.

Answer: (C) Recognised as expense in Profit and Loss annually — routine maintenance/updation merely preserves the asset's existing level of performance rather than generating benefits in excess of its originally assessed performance, so it fails AS 26's subsequent-expenditure capitalisation test and is expensed as incurred.

MTP Sep 2024 Set 2 · Part II · Q1(b) · Marks: 4 · Compulsory

A company incorporated in June 2023, has setup a factory within a period of 8 months with borrowed funds. The construction period of the assets had reduced drastically due to usage of technical innovations by the company and the company is able to justify the reasons for the same. Whether interest on borrowings for the period prior to the date of setting up the factory should be capitalized although it has taken less than 12 months for the assets to get ready for use. You are required to comment on the necessary treatment with reference to AS 16.

As per AS 16 ‘Borrowing Costs’, a qualifying asset is one that necessarily takes a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended use or sale. What constitutes a substantial period of time primarily depends on the facts and circumstances of each case; ordinarily a period of twelve months is considered substantial unless a shorter or longer period can be justified. In estimating the period, the time an asset takes, technologically and commercially, to get ready for its intended use or sale is considered — there is a rebuttable presumption that 12 months constitutes a substantial period.

Under circumstances where the construction period has reduced drastically due to technical innovation, the 12-month period should be looked at as a benchmark, not a conclusive yardstick. An enterprise that completes the asset in 8 months should not be penalised for its efficiency by being denied interest capitalisation. The substantial-period criterion exists to ensure enterprises do not spend disproportionate time and effort capturing immaterial interest cost for capitalisation purposes.

Conclusion: since the factory was constructed in 8 months (a genuinely justified, technically-innovation-driven reduction), it is still considered a qualifying asset, and the interest on borrowings for the same shall be capitalised, although it took less than 12 months for the asset to get ready for use.

AS 16 (4.5)
A common error here is treating the 12-month benchmark as a rigid, non-rebuttable rule and denying capitalisation outright once construction takes under 12 months, without weighing the facts-and-circumstances test AS 16 actually applies.

As per AS 12 ‘Accounting for Government Grants’, income from a Deferred Grant Account is allocated to Profit and Loss usually over the periods and in the proportions in which depreciation on the related asset is charged. In the first two years, (₹32 lakhs/4 years) = ₹8 lakhs × 2 years = ₹16 lakhs is credited to P&L, leaving ₹16 lakhs as the balance of the Deferred Grant Account after two years.   To Bank A/c32
(Being Government grant refunded)

1. Value of fixed assets after two years but before refund of grant: initial cost ₹80 lakhs; depreciation per year = (₹80 lakhs − ₹8 lakhs)/4 years = ₹18 lakhs; book value after two years = 80 − (18×2) = ₹44 lakhs.

2. Value of fixed assets after refund of grant: on refund the Deferred Grant Account balance becomes nil, but the fixed asset continues to be shown in the books at ₹44 lakhs (unchanged — the refund does not restate the asset's carrying amount).

MTP Sep 2024 Set 2 · Part II · Q6(a), alternative 1 of 2 (OR) · Marks: 4

“Accounting Standards standardize diverse accounting policies with a view to eliminate the non-comparability of financial statements and improve the reliability of financial statements.” Discuss and explain the benefits of Accounting Standards.

Accounting Standards standardize diverse accounting policies with a view to eliminating non-comparability of financial statements and improving the reliability of financial statements. They provide a set of standard accounting policies, valuation norms, and disclosure requirements, aiming at improving the quality of financial reporting by promoting comparability, consistency, and transparency in the interests of users of financial statements.

Benefits of Accounting Standards:

(i) Standardization of alternative accounting treatments: Accounting Standards reduce, to a reasonable extent, confusing variations in the accounting treatment used to prepare financial statements.

(ii) Requirements for additional disclosures: there are certain areas where important information is not statutorily required to be disclosed; Standards may call for disclosure beyond that required by law.

(iii) Comparability of financial statements: applying accounting standards facilitates comparison of financial statements of different companies situated in India, and to a limited extent, of companies situated in different parts of the world — though differences in institutions, traditions, and legal systems from one country to another still give rise to differences in the Accounting Standards adopted in different countries.

Introduction to Accounting Standards (1)
A common error here is listing only one or two benefits instead of covering the full range (standardisation, additional disclosure, comparability), or omitting the caveat that cross-country comparability remains limited by differing legal/institutional environments.

Amount required for 10% dividend on ₹80,00,000 = ₹8,00,000. Profit available for the current year = ₹1,42,500. Amount to be utilized from reserves = ₹8,00,000 − ₹1,42,500 = ₹6,57,500.

Conditions as per Companies (Declaration and Payment of Dividend) Rules, 2014:

Condition I: since the proposed rate (10%) is lower than the average rate of dividend over the last five years (12%), a 10% dividend can be declared.

Condition II: the maximum amount that can be drawn from accumulated profits and reserves must not exceed 10% of paid-up capital plus free reserves = 10% × (₹80,00,000 + ₹25,00,000) = ₹10,50,000 — the required ₹6,57,500 is within this cap.

Condition III: the balance of reserves after the drawal, ₹18,42,500 (₹25,00,000 − ₹6,57,500), must not fall below 15% of paid-up capital, i.e. ₹12,00,000 (15% of ₹80,00,000) — satisfied.

All three conditions being satisfied, XYZ Ltd. can withdraw ₹6,57,500 from the accumulated General Reserve. (Revaluation Reserve, not being a free reserve, is not counted in this computation.)

A common error here is including the Revaluation Reserve as part of free reserves when applying the three conditions, when it is explicitly excluded because it represents an unrealised, notional gain.
