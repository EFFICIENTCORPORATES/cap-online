ADI Ltd (the Company), engaged in the business of manufacturing of urea, has set up its business in a designated backward area which entitles the company to receive from the Government of India a subsidy of 20% of the cost of investment.

Having fulfilled all the conditions under the scheme, the Company on its overall investment of ₹50 crores received ₹10 crores from the Government in January 2024 (financial year being 2023-24). The Company wants to treat this receipt as an item of revenue and thereby reduce the losses on profit and loss account for the year ended 31 March 2024.

ADI Ltd holds 51% in SHA Ltd. SHA Ltd is a joint venture of ADI Ltd due to a contractual agreement. ADI Ltd is engaged in the manufacturing business and it entered into a joint venture to get synergies in the same business. ADI Ltd and SHA Ltd hold 10% and 30% respectively in SHB Ltd.

As per the requirements of SEBI, ADI Ltd prepared results/accounts for the quarter ended 30 June 2024 and took following positions in respect of following accounting matters:

  1. Training expenses incurred during the quarter ended 30 June 2024 were allocated equally over the four quarters because the benefit was spread over the entire year and similarly some training expenses expected to be incurred in the last quarter have been estimated and equally allocated over the four quarters.
  2. Provision made for donation of ₹5 million expected to be made in the second quarter.
  3. Since historically there has been an immaterial variance between budgets and actuals, depreciation charge for the quarter was determined by the budgeted figure.
  4. Incentives were provided to the customers if they purchase 1 million kgs of urea on an annual basis. It was expected that at least 50 customers would be able to achieve this target before the end of third quarter. No provision was made for this incentive during the quarter ended 30 June 2024 since ADI Ltd believed that the provision was not yet fructified.

ADI Ltd owns 60% holding in ANI Ltd, an unquoted entity. The government has recently announced an increase in interest rates. The increase will cause a fall in value of equity holdings. This is due to the fact that risk free investments offer a higher return making them relatively more attractive. The market value of equity will adjust downwards to improve the return available on this sort of investment.

SHB Ltd took a loan of USD 10,000 on 1 April 2023 for a specific project at an interest rate of 5% p.a. payable annually. On 1 April 2023, the exchange rate between the currency was ₹81 per USD. The exchange rate as at 31 March 2024 was ₹82 per USD. The corresponding amount could have been borrowed by SHB Ltd in local currency at an interest rate of 11% per annum as on 1 April 2023.

ADI Ltd follows April to March as the financial year.

MTP Sep 2025 Set 2 · Part I – Case Scenario I · Q1 · Marks: 2

In respect of abovementioned receipt of ₹10 crores, which of the following options would be correct under Indian GAAP?

  1. Accounting treatment desired by the company is correct.
  2. The subsidy should be credited to capital reserve.
  3. The subsidy should be treated as a capital grant.
  4. The accounting treatment should be as per the accounting policy of the company in relation to any grant. The company can choose any accounting policy in this respect.

Answer: (B) — the subsidy is calculated as 20% of the Company's total investment (₹50 crores), not tied to the cost of any one specific depreciable fixed asset, and is received only after the Company fulfils backward-area investment conditions. This is the classic AS 12 fact pattern for a "government grant of the nature of promoters' contribution" — such a grant is credited directly to Capital Reserve and treated as part of shareholders' funds, never recognised as revenue/P&L income (so the Company's own desired treatment, option (A), is wrong).

AS 12 (1.8)
A common error here is treating any government receipt tied to fixed-asset investment as automatically amortisable "deferred income," without first checking whether the grant is actually keyed to total capital investment (a promoters'-contribution grant, credited straight to Capital Reserve) rather than to one identifiable asset.

Please suggest which one of the following options would be correct in respect of consolidated accounts of ADI Ltd as per Accounting Standards notified under the Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules.

  1. In the consolidated accounts of ADI Ltd, it would account for its 10% investment in SHB Ltd as per AS 13 and 30% investment of SHA Ltd in SHB Ltd would be accounted for using equity method.
  2. In the consolidated accounts of ADI Ltd, it would account for its 10% investment in SHB Ltd as per equity method and 30% investment of SHA Ltd in SHB Ltd would be accounted using proportionate consolidation method.
  3. In the consolidated accounts of ADI Ltd, it would account for its 10% investment in SHB Ltd as per AS 13 and 30% investment of SHA Ltd in SHB Ltd would be accounted using proportionate consolidation method.
  4. In the consolidated accounts of ADI Ltd, it would account for its 10% investment in SHB Ltd as per equity method and 30% investment of SHA Ltd in SHB Ltd would also be accounted for using equity method.

Answer: (D) — at the ADI Group's consolidated level, ADI's own direct 10% stake and SHA's 30% stake in SHB Ltd represent a single reporting entity's combined ownership interest in SHB (10% + 51% economic interest in SHA's 30% ≈ 25%+ effective exposure), which crosses the 20% significant-influence threshold in aggregate, with SHA's board seats and business synergies giving the wider group real participation in SHB's policy decisions. On that basis, SHB is treated as an associate of the ADI Group as a whole and equity-accounted for both stakes in the consolidated statements.

AS 23 (2.3)
A frequent slip is assessing each group entity's stake in SHB in isolation (10% here, 30% there) rather than recognising that, for the purpose of preparing one set of consolidated financial statements, the reporting group's combined exposure is what determines whether significant influence exists.

MTP Sep 2025 Set 2 · Part I – Case Scenario I · Q3 · Marks: 2

Please share your views in respect of the accounting positions taken by ADI Ltd (Points i to iv) as per Accounting Standards notified under the Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules.

  1. Position taken in point ii was incorrect.
  2. Positions taken in points ii and iv were incorrect.
  3. Positions taken in points i and ii were incorrect.
  4. Positions taken in points i, ii, iii and iv were incorrect.

Answer: (D) — all four interim-quarter positions are flawed: (i) training costs create no asset, so smoothing them (including estimated future-quarter training cost) equally across all four quarters is not appropriate merely because "benefit is spread over the year"; (ii) a donation not yet made, with no present obligation existing at quarter-end, cannot be provided for in advance of the obligating event; (iii) interim depreciation must be computed on the actual asset base for the period, not substituted with a budgeted figure, however historically close budget-to-actual has been; (iv) since the Company itself expects at least 50 customers to hit the volume target before quarter three, a proportionate share of the expected incentive cost linked to sales already made in the quarter should be accrued as those sales occur, not deferred until the target has "fructified."

A common error here is assuming that any expense with a "spread benefit" or any provision management is reasonably confident about may be smoothed or anticipated at will in interim accounts — AS 25 still requires each item to independently satisfy its own recognition test for the interim period, not a general fairness-of-spreading argument.

MTP Sep 2025 Set 2 · Part I – Case Scenario I · Q4 · Marks: 2

Will there be any adjustment required in the financial statements of ADI Ltd because of abovementioned event (increase in interest rates by the Government) as per Accounting Standards notified under the Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules.

  1. The increase is an indication that ADI Ltd's holding in ANI Ltd might have increased. Hence ADI Ltd would require to increase the value of its interest in ANI Ltd.
  2. No adjustment is required.
  3. The increase is an indication that ADI Ltd's holding in ANI Ltd might be impaired. ADI Ltd should make a formal estimate of the recoverable amount of its interest in ANI Ltd.
  4. Whether to make any adjustment or not will depend on the accounting policy of ADI Ltd.

Answer: (C) — a rise in interest rates makes competing risk-free investments relatively more attractive, pushing the market value of an unquoted equity holding like ANI Ltd downward. This is a classic external indicator of impairment under AS 28: it does not automatically write anything down, but it does trigger a duty to formally estimate the recoverable amount of the interest and test for impairment.

AS 28 (7.3)
Some candidates read "increase in interest rates" as good news for the holding company and reached for option (A), missing that a rate rise makes the *investment's own* market value fall relative to newly-attractive risk-free alternatives — the direction of the impairment indicator is easy to get backwards under exam time pressure.

MTP Sep 2025 Set 2 · Part I – Case Scenario II · Q6 · Marks: 2

In respect of abovementioned land in residential zone, what would be the accounting treatment under Indian GAAP?

  1. The 'land in residential zone' appearing in property, plant and equipment would be credited by its carrying amount and inventory would be recognised equivalent to its fair value. Differential will be taken to profit or loss on conversion.
  2. The 'land in residential zone' appearing in property, plant and equipment would be credited by its gross amount and inventory would be recognised equivalent to carrying amount of reclassified property, plant and equipment. Differential will be taken to profit or loss on conversion.
  3. The 'land in residential zone' appearing in property, plant and equipment would be credited by its carrying amount and inventory would be recognised at the same amount.
  4. The 'land in residential zone' appearing in property, plant and equipment would be credited by its gross amount and inventory would be recognised at the same amount.

Answer: (C) — once management's intention changes to selling the plotted land rather than using it operationally, it is reclassified from PPE to inventory. Reclassification itself is not a disposal or a remeasurement event, so no gain/loss is recognised at the point of transfer — the land leaves PPE at its existing carrying amount and enters inventory at that same amount; only on eventual sale is any profit recognised.

AS 10 (2.3)
Some candidates step up the reclassified land to fair value and route the uplift through profit or loss, treating the change of intended use itself as a realisation event — reclassification carries the existing carrying amount across unchanged; only a subsequent sale realises any gain.

What should be the accounting treatment in the statement of profit and loss of RIMMI Ltd in year 1 in respect of property, plant and equipment?

  1. Overhauling costs of ₹5 crores and depreciation of ₹1 crore would be charged to the statement of profit and loss in year 1.
  2. Depreciation of ₹2 crores would be charged to the statement of profit and loss in year 1.
  3. Overhauling costs of ₹5 crores and depreciation of ₹75 lakhs would be charged to the statement of profit and loss in year 1.
  4. Depreciation of ₹1.75 crores would be charged to the statement of profit and loss in year 1.

Answer: (D) ₹1.75 crores — per AS 10's component approach, the anticipated first-overhaul cost of ₹5 crores is carved out as a separate component of the vessel with its own 5-year useful life (to the next overhaul), depreciated at ₹5cr ÷ 5 = ₹1 crore/year. The remaining vessel component (₹20cr − ₹5cr = ₹15cr) is depreciated over the full 20-year life at ₹15cr ÷ 20 = ₹0.75 crore/year. Total Year 1 depreciation = ₹1 crore + ₹0.75 crore = ₹1.75 crores; no ₹5 crore expense is charged in Year 1 since the actual overhaul has not yet occurred, only its component is being depreciated in advance.

AS 10 (2.10)
A common error here is expensing the anticipated overhaul cost outright in the year it is estimated (options (A)/(C)), rather than recognising it as a distinct depreciable component depreciated over its own shorter useful life alongside the main asset.

In the books of G Ltd., closing inventory as at 31.03.2024 amounts to ₹10,40,000 (on the basis of FIFO method). The company decides to change from FIFO method to weighted average method for ascertaining the cost of inventory for 31.3.2024. On the basis of weighted average method, closing inventory as on 31.03.2024 amounts to ₹8,80,000. Realisable value of the inventory as on 31.03.2024 amounts to ₹12,00,000. What will be the value of inventory in the books and what disclosure should be given in the financial statement on 31.3.2024?

  1. The value of inventory will be ₹8,80,000 and the fact that the valuation method has changed to be disclosed in the financial statement.
  2. The value of inventory will be ₹12,00,000, and full disclosure with the amount the valuation method has changed to be disclosed in the financial statement.
  3. The value of inventory will be ₹12,00,000, and the fact that valuation method has changed to be disclosed in the financial statement.
  4. The value of inventory will be ₹8,80,000, and full disclosure with the amount the valuation method has changed to be disclosed in the financial statement.

Answer: (D) — inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value; under the newly-adopted weighted-average method, cost is ₹8,80,000, which is lower than the ₹12,00,000 NRV, so ₹8,80,000 is the value carried in the books. A common error here is picking the correct ₹8,80,000 value but under-specifying the required disclosure as merely "the fact of change" (option (A)) rather than the fuller disclosure AS 1 actually requires for a change in accounting policy — the fact alone is not enough, the amount of the effect must also be disclosed.

In the financial statements, Asha Ltd. wants to present Sasha company as a related party. Comment on the disclosure requirement.

(i) As per AS 18, parties are considered related if at any time during the reporting period one party has the ability to control the other or exercise significant influence over the other. Transactions of ABC Ltd. with its associate company for the first quarter ending 30.06.2025 only are required to be disclosed as related party transactions, as the company had the ability to exercise significant influence only until 30.6.2025. The transactions for the period in which the related party relationship did not exist need not be reported.

(ii) Arjun Ltd. cannot be said to control the composition of the board of directors of Bheem Ltd., as the directors have been appointed in their individual capacity as professionals and not by virtue of their being directors in Arjun Ltd. Hence, it cannot be concluded that the companies are related merely because the majority of the directors of one company became the majority of the directors of the second in their individual capacity as professionals.

(iii) In the context of AS 18, a single customer, supplier, franchiser, distributor, or general agent with whom an enterprise transacts a significant volume of business cannot be construed as a Related Party Relationship merely by virtue of the resulting economic dependence. There is economic dependence between the companies, but no one controls or exercises significant influence on the other. Asha Ltd. need not report Sasha Company as its related party in its financial statements.

AS 18 (4.3/4.4)
A common error here is over-extending "related party" to any relationship of economic significance — mere economic dependence on a sole customer, or directors overlapping only in an unrelated individual professional capacity, does not by itself create control or significant influence under AS 18.

How will interest be capitalised when qualifying assets are funded by borrowings in the nature of bonds that are issued at a discount?

As per AS 16, "Borrowing costs are interest and other costs incurred by an enterprise in connection with the borrowing of funds." The Statement also contemplates "amortisation of discounts or premiums relating to borrowings" as a component of borrowing costs (paragraph 4(b)). Thus, borrowing costs comprise the periodic interest payable on the bonds in question and the amount of discount amortised during the period.

Paragraph 6 of the Statement states that "Borrowing costs that are directly attributable to the acquisition, construction or production of a qualifying asset should be capitalised as part of the cost of that asset." Paragraph 19 further states that "Capitalisation of borrowing costs should cease when substantially all the activities necessary to prepare the qualifying asset for its intended use or sale are complete." Thus, only that portion of the amortised discount should be capitalised as part of the cost of a qualifying asset which relates to the period during which acquisition, construction or production of the asset takes place.

AS 16 (4.4)
A common error here is treating only the coupon interest as the borrowing cost and overlooking that the periodic amortisation of a bond's issue discount is itself an explicit component of borrowing cost under AS 16.

Classify the items as current or non-current assets in accordance with Accounting Standard 1 (AS 1) and, where AS 1 is silent, refer to Schedule III of the Companies Act, 2013: Security deposits paid to electricity company; Raw materials inventory; Prepaid rent (for 14 months); Fixed deposits with 18-month maturity.
