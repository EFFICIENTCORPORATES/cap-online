RTP May 2025 · Part I – MCQs based on Case Scenarios · Q1

In the books of G Ltd., closing inventory as at 31.03.2024 amounts to ₹10,40,000 (on the basis of FIFO method). The company decides to change from FIFO method to weighted average method for ascertaining the cost of inventory for 31.3.2024. On the basis of weighted average method, closing inventory as on 31.03.2024 amounts to ₹8,80,000. Realisable value of the inventory as on 31.03.2024 amounts to ₹12,00,000. What will be the value of inventory in the books and what disclosure should be given in the financial statement on 31.3.2024?

  1. The value of inventory will be ₹8,80,000 and the fact that the valuation method has changed to be disclosed in the financial statement.
  2. The value of inventory will be ₹12,00,000, and full disclosure with the amount the valuation method has changed to be disclosed in the financial statement.
  3. The value of inventory will be ₹12,00,000, and the fact that valuation method has changed to be disclosed in the financial statement.
  4. The value of inventory will be ₹8,80,000, and full disclosure with the amount the valuation method has changed to be disclosed in the financial statement.

Answer: (d) The value of inventory will be ₹8,80,000, and full disclosure with the amount the valuation method has changed to be disclosed in the financial statement. Per AS 2, inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value; once the company changes its costing method to weighted average, the "cost" figure to compare against NRV is the new weighted-average cost of ₹8,80,000, which is lower than the ₹12,00,000 NRV – so ₹8,80,000 is the value in the books. Separately, per AS 5, a change in the cost formula used for inventories is a change in accounting policy, which requires disclosure of both the fact of the change and, where the effect is material, the amount by which the change affects the financial statements.

AS 2 — Valuation of Inventories (1.3)
A common error here is stopping at "lower of cost and NRV" and picking ₹8,80,000 without also recognising that switching the costing method itself is a change in accounting policy requiring full disclosure of the amount involved, not merely a passing mention that the method changed.

RTP May 2025 · Part I – MCQs based on Case Scenarios · Q2

A Ltd. has a balance of ₹17,15,000 in the loan account with State Finance Corporation which is inclusive of ₹1,15,000 for interest accrued but not due. The loan is secured by hypothecation of the Plant and Machinery. As per Schedule III to the Companies Act, 2013 loan is to be disclosed in the balance sheet as follows:

  1. Disclosed ₹16,00,000 as a secured loan under long-term borrowings.
  2. Disclosed ₹16,00,000 as a secured loan under long-term borrowings and ₹1,15,000 under short-term borrowings.
  3. Disclosed ₹16,00,000 as a secured loan under long-term borrowings and ₹1,15,000 under other current liabilities.
  4. Disclosed ₹16,00,000 as a secured loan under long-term borrowings and no disclosure for ₹1,15,000.

Answer: (c) Disclosed ₹16,00,000 as a secured loan under long-term borrowings and ₹1,15,000 under other current liabilities. The principal loan balance (₹17,15,000 − ₹1,15,000 interest accrued but not due = ₹16,00,000) is a long-term secured borrowing. Per Schedule III's format, "interest accrued but not due on borrowings" is not itself a borrowing (long-term or short-term) – it is separately classified under "Other current liabilities."

Preparation of Financial Statements (1.5)
A frequent mistake is treating "interest accrued but not due" as part of either the long-term or short-term borrowings line itself, rather than recognising Schedule III's specific separate classification for it under Other Current Liabilities.

RTP May 2025 · Part I – General MCQ · Q3

Most by-products as well as scrap or waste materials, by their nature, are immaterial. Thus, these are measured at:

  1. Cost
  2. Cost or Net Realisable Value whichever is lower
  3. Nil
  4. Net realisable value

Answer: (d) Net realisable value. Per AS 2, because by-products, scrap and waste are immaterial by nature, they are measured at net realisable value, and this NRV is deducted from the cost of the main product(s) rather than being separately costed.

AS 2 — Valuation of Inventories (1.7)
A common error is answering "cost or NRV whichever is lower" by analogy to the general inventory rule, missing that AS 2 carves out a specific, simpler NRV-only rule for immaterial by-products, scrap and waste.

RTP May 2025 · Part II · Q4

What do you mean by 'Accrual' in reference to AS-1? Also, specify any three reasons for 'Accrual Basis of Accounting'.

The term "Accrual" has been explained in AS 1 on Disclosure of Accounting Policies, as "Revenues and costs are accrued, that is, recognised as they are earned or incurred (and not as money is received or paid) and recorded in the financial statements of the periods to which they relate."

Reasons for Accrual Basis of Accounting (any three):

  1. Accrual basis of accounting attempts to record the financial effects of the transactions, events, and circumstances of an enterprise in the period in which they occur, rather than recording them in the period(s) in which cash is received or paid by the enterprise.
  2. Receipts and payments of the period will not coincide with the buying, producing or selling events and other economic events that affect entity performance.
  3. The goal of accrual basis of accounting is to follow the matching concept of income and expenditure so that reported net income measures an enterprise's performance during a period instead of merely listing its cash receipts and payments.
  4. Accrual basis of accounting recognizes assets, liabilities or components of revenues and expenses for amounts received or paid in cash in the past, and amounts expected to be received or paid in cash in the future.
  5. An important point of difference between accrual accounting and accounting based on cash receipts and outlay is the timing of recognition of revenues, expenses, gains and losses.

RTP May 2025 · Part II · Q7

Smart Ltd. closes its books of accounts every year on 31st March. The financial statements for the year ended 31st March, 2024 are to be approved by the approving authority on 30th June 2024. During the first quarter of 2024-2025, the following events/transactions have taken place. The accountant of the company seeks your guidance for the following:

  1. Smart Ltd. has an inventory of 50 stitching machines costing at ₹5,500 per machine as on 31st March, 2024. The company is expecting a heavy decline in the demand in next year. The inventories are valued at cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower. During the month of April 2024, due to fall in demand, the prices have gone down drastically. The company has sold 5 machines during this month at a price of ₹4,000 per machine.
  2. A fire has broken out in the company's godown on 15th April, 2024. The company has estimated a loss of ₹25 lakhs of which 75% is recoverable from the Insurance company.
  3. The company has entered into a sale agreement on 30th March, 2024 to sell a property for a consideration of ₹7,50,000 which is being carried in the books at ₹5,50,000 at the year end. The transfer of risk and reward and sale is complete in the month of May 2024 when conveyance and possession get completed.
  4. The company has received, during the year 2022-2023, a government grant of ₹15 lakhs for purchase of a machine. The company has received a notice for refund of the said grant on 15th June, 2024 due to violation of some of the conditions of grant during the year 2023-2024.

You are required to state with reasons, how the above transactions will be dealt with in the financial statement for the year ended 31st March 2024.

Events occurring after the balance sheet date are significant events, favourable or unfavourable, occurring between the balance sheet date and the date on which the financial statements are approved. Assets and liabilities should be adjusted for such events only if they provide additional evidence of conditions existing at the balance sheet date, or indicate the going-concern assumption is no longer appropriate. Here, the financial statements are approved on 30th June 2024.

  1. Since on 31st March 2024 Smart Ltd. was already expecting a heavy decline in demand, the price fall during April 2024 is considered an adjusting event, providing further evidence of the condition (declining net realisable value) existing at the balance sheet date. Inventory should be written down to ₹4,000 per machine: 50 machines × ₹4,000 = ₹2,00,000.
  2. The fire took place after the balance sheet date (during FY 2024-25), so the financials for FY 2023-24 are not adjusted for the fire loss. However, the going-concern assumption should be evaluated in light of the fire; if it remains appropriate, no disclosure is required in the financial statements.
  3. Since the transfer of risk and reward and completion of sale occurred only in May 2024 (conveyance and possession), no revenue should be recognised for this in the FY 2023-24 financial statements – this is a non-adjusting event, though disclosure of the agreement should be given.
  4. Since the refund notice was received after 31st March but before the 30th June 2024 approval date, and the violation of grant conditions took place during FY 2023-24 (a condition the company must have been aware of), the grant refund is an adjusting event and shall be adjusted in the FY 2023-24 financial statements.
AS 4 — Contingencies and Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date (1.4/1.5)
A common error is treating item (i)'s post-year-end price fall as a non-adjusting event because the sale itself happened after the year end, missing that the company's own pre-year-end expectation of a demand decline is what makes the subsequent price evidence adjusting rather than the sale transaction itself.

RTP May 2025 · Part II · Q8

The Accountant of Heera Ltd. has sought your opinion with relevant reasons, whether the following transactions will be treated as change in Accounting Policy or not for the year ended 31st March, 2024. Please advise him in the following situations in accordance with the provisions of relevant Accounting Standard:

  1. Till the previous year the furniture was depreciated on straight line basis over a period of 5 years. From current year, the useful life of furniture has been changed to 3 years.
  2. Management decided to pay pension to those employees who have retired after completing 5 years of service in the organization. Such employees will get pension of ₹20,000 per month. Earlier there was no such scheme of pension in the organization.
  3. During the year ended 31st March, 2024, there was change in cost formula in measuring the cost of inventories.
  1. Change in useful life of furniture from 5 years to 3 years is a change in accounting estimate, not a change in accounting policy.
  2. Adoption of a new accounting policy for events or transactions which did not occur previously (there was no pension scheme before) should not be treated as a change in an accounting policy. The introduction of the new pension scheme is therefore not a change in accounting policy.
  3. Change in the cost formula used in measuring the cost of inventories is a change in accounting policy.
AS 5 (2.5)
A common error is classifying the new pension scheme as a change in accounting policy simply because it is a new accounting treatment being adopted, missing AS 5's specific rule that adopting a policy for an event that did not previously occur is not a change in an existing policy.

RTP May 2025 · Part II · Q9(i)

AP Ltd., a construction contractor, undertakes the construction of a commercial complex for Kay Ltd. AP Ltd. submitted separate proposals for each of 3 units of the commercial complex. A single agreement is entered into between the two parties. The agreement lays down the value of each of the 3 units, i.e. ₹50 Lakh, ₹60 Lakh and ₹75 Lakh respectively. The agreement also lays down the completion time for each unit. Comment, with reference to AS-7, whether AP Ltd. should treat it as a single contract or three separate contracts.

As per AS 7 'Construction Contracts', when a contract covers a number of assets, the construction of each asset should be treated as a separate construction contract when: (a) separate proposals have been submitted for each asset; (b) each asset has been subject to separate negotiation and the contractor and customer have been able to accept or reject that part of the contract relating to each asset; and (c) the costs and revenues of each asset can be identified.

In the given case, AP Ltd. submitted separate proposals for each of the 3 units, the agreement lays down a separate value and separate completion time for each unit, and the costs/revenues of each unit are identifiable. Therefore, AP Ltd. is required to treat the construction of each unit as a separate construction contract, as the above-mentioned conditions of AS 7 are fulfilled in the given case – notwithstanding that a single agreement document was used.

AS 7 — Construction Contracts (1.3)
A common error is concluding it must be a single contract simply because only one agreement document was signed, missing that AS 7 looks at the three specific conditions (separate proposals, separate negotiability, identifiable costs/revenues) rather than the number of physical documents.

RTP May 2025 · Part II · Q10

Given below are the following information of B.S. Ltd.:

  1. Goods of ₹50,000 were sold on 18-03-2024 but at the request of the buyer these were delivered on 15-04-2024.
  2. On 13-01-2024 goods of ₹1,25,000 are sent on consignment basis, of which 20% of the goods unsold are lying with the consignee as on 31-03-2024.
  3. ₹1,00,000 worth of goods were sold on approval basis on 01-12-2023. The period of approval was 3 months after which they were considered sold. Buyer sent approval for 75% goods up to 31-01-2024 and no approval or disapproval received for the remaining goods till 31-03-2024.

You are required to advise the accountant of B.S. Ltd., with valid reasons, the amount to be recognized as revenue for the year ended 31st March, 2024 in the above cases in the context of AS-9.

As per AS 9 'Revenue Recognition', in a transaction involving the sale of goods, performance should be regarded as being achieved when: (a) the seller has transferred to the buyer the property in the goods for a price, or all significant risks and rewards of ownership have been transferred and the seller retains no effective control of the goods to a degree usually associated with ownership; and (b) no significant uncertainty exists regarding the amount of consideration to be derived from the sale.

Case (i): The sale is complete but delivery has been postponed at the buyer's request. B.S. Ltd. should recognize the entire sale of ₹50,000 for the year ended 31st March, 2024.

Case (ii): In case of a consignment sale, revenue should not be recognized until the goods are sold by the consignee to a third party. The 20% of goods lying unsold with the consignee should be treated as closing inventory, and sales should be recognized for ₹1,00,000 (80% of ₹1,25,000).

Case (iii): In case of goods sold on approval basis, revenue should not be recognized until the goods have been formally accepted by the buyer, or the buyer has done an act adopting the transaction, or the time period for rejection has elapsed (or, where no time has been fixed, a reasonable time has elapsed). Since the 3-month approval period had already expired by 31st March, 2024, revenue should be recognized for the full ₹1,00,000 (including the portion neither approved nor disapproved, since the approval window itself had lapsed).

Thus, total revenue amounting to ₹2,50,000 (50,000 + 1,00,000 + 1,00,000) will be recognized for the year ended 31st March, 2024 in the books of B.S. Ltd.

AS 9 — Revenue Recognition (2.8)
A common error in case (iii) is withholding revenue recognition on the un-responded-to 25% of goods on the reasoning that no explicit acceptance was received, missing that AS 9 treats the mere expiry of the stated rejection period as itself sufficient for revenue recognition, with no separate acceptance needed.

RTP May 2025 · Part II · Q13

XYZ Limited has set-up its business in a designated backward area which entitles the company to receive from the Government of India a subsidy of 20% of the cost of investment. Having fulfilled all the conditions under the scheme, the company on its investment of ₹75 crore received ₹15 crore as subsidy from the Government in January 2024. The company wants to treat this receipt as an item of revenue and thereby reduce the losses for the year ended on 31st March, 2024. Keeping in view the relevant Accounting Standard, examine whether this action is justified or not.

As per para 10 of AS 12 'Accounting for Government Grants', where government grants are in the nature of promoters' contribution – i.e. they are given with reference to the total investment in an undertaking, or by way of contribution towards its total capital outlay, and no repayment is ordinarily expected in respect thereof – the grants are treated as Capital Reserve, which can be neither distributed as dividend nor considered as deferred income.

In the given case, the subsidy received (20% of the cost of investment) is neither in relation to a specific fixed asset nor in relation to revenue – it is linked to the total investment made in the backward-area undertaking. It is therefore inappropriate to recognise this government grant in the profit and loss statement, since it is not earned but represents an incentive provided by the government without related costs.

The correct treatment is to credit the ₹15 crore subsidy to Capital Reserve. Therefore, the accounting treatment (treating it as revenue to reduce the reported loss) followed by the company is not proper.

AS 12 — Accounting for Government Grants (1.8)
A common error is treating any cash subsidy received as revenue income by default, without first testing whether it meets AS 12's specific "promoters' contribution" characterisation (tied to total investment, not a specific asset or specific revenue item), which mandates Capital Reserve treatment instead.

The company also has an option to sub-let the site at a rent of ₹45,000 per month.
  2. A case has been filed against the company in the consumer court and a notice for levy of a penalty of ₹20 lakhs has been received. The company has appointed a lawyer to defend the case for a fee of ₹2 lakhs. 50% of the fees has been paid and the balance 50% will be paid after finalisation of the case. There are 75% chances that the penalty may not be levied.
  3. The company had committed to supply a consignment worth ₹1 crore to one of its dealers by the year-end. As per the contract, if delivery is not made on time, a compensation of 15% of the value is to be paid. While the consignment was in transit, one of the trucks carrying goods worth ₹30 lakhs met with an accident. It was however covered by Insurance. According to the surveyor's report, the policy amount is collectable, subject to 10% deduction. Before closing the books of accounts, the company has received the information that the policy amount has been processed and the dealer has also claimed for the compensation.

Give your answers based on relevant Accounting Standard.

(i) As per AS 29, an 'onerous contract' is a contract in which the unavoidable costs of meeting the obligations under it exceed the economic benefits expected to be received under it; the unavoidable cost is the lower of the net cost of fulfilling the contract and any compensation/penalties from failing to fulfil it. Here: cost of cancelling the agreement = ₹80,000 × 6 months = ₹4,80,000. Cost of continuing the agreement while sub-letting = net of rent paid over rent collected from sub-tenant = (₹80,000 − ₹45,000) × 12 months = ₹4,20,000. The lower of the two, ₹4,20,000, is the amount to be provided for.

(ii) As per AS 29, an obligation is a present obligation only if, based on available evidence, its existence at the balance sheet date is probable (more likely than not). Here, there are 75% chances the penalty may not be levied – so the probability of an outflow is below the "more likely than not" threshold, and Saharsh Ltd. should not make a provision for the ₹20 lakh penalty. However, a provision should be made for the remaining 50% (unpaid) lawyer's fee, since that liability is not contingent on the outcome of the case.

(iii) Loss due to accident = ₹30,00,000. Insurance claim receivable by the company = ₹30,00,000 × 90% = ₹27,00,000 (10% deduction per the surveyor's report). Loss to be recognised in the books for 2023-24 = ₹30,00,000 − ₹27,00,000 = ₹3,00,000. Insurance claim receivable recorded in the books = ₹27,00,000 (virtually certain, per the processed policy amount). Compensation claim by the dealer, to be provided for in the books = ₹30,00,000 × 15% = ₹4,50,000.
Note (per source): alternatively, the 15% compensation can be computed on the whole ₹1 crore contract value, giving ₹15,00,000 (₹1 crore × 15%) instead.

A common error in part (ii) is providing for the full ₹20 lakh penalty out of caution despite the stated 75% chance it will not be levied, missing that AS 29's "more likely than not" threshold is a specific probability test, not a general prudence override.
