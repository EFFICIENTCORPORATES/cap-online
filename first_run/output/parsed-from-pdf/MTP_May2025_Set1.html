MTP May 2025 Set 1 · Part I – Case Scenario I · Q1 · Marks: 2

What is the maximum number of shares to be bought back as per debt-equity ratio?

  1. 1,12,000 shares
  2. 80,000 shares
  3. 54,000 shares
  4. 60,000 shares

Answer: (B) 80,000 shares.

Buy-back of Securities (1)
A common error here is computing the Debt-Equity Ratio Test as a single-step "present equity minus minimum equity" subtraction, which ignores that the nominal value of shares bought back must also be carved out of the free-reserves base (transferred to Capital Redemption Reserve) before the maximum buy-back amount can be solved — this requires the simultaneous-equation approach shown in ICAI's own worked illustrations, not a direct subtraction.
Options verbatim from CAInter-AdvAcc-MTP-May2025-Set1-Q.pdf; the Ans.pdf answer key gives only "1. (i) (b)" with no working shown anywhere for Q1's four sub-parts. Independently re-derived per the ICAI-standard simultaneous-equation method (see M3_C12_U1_ Buyback of Securities.md, Illustration 5): Present equity funds (paid-up capital ₹24,00,000 + free reserves ₹30,00,000 [General Reserve+Securities Premium+P&L; Infrastructure Development Reserve and Revaluation Reserve excluded as not "free"] = ₹54,00,000); minimum equity to be maintained (Debt ₹52,00,000 ÷ 2) = ₹26,00,000. Let y = maximum permissible buy-back amount (₹), x = nominal value transferred to CRR = (y÷25)×10 = 0.4y. Equation: (54,00,000 − x) − 26,00,000 = y ⇒ 28,00,000 = 1.4y ⇒ y = ₹20,00,000 ⇒ shares = 20,00,000÷25 = 80,000, reproducing option (B) exactly and cross-checking consistently against this same case's parts (ii) and (iii) below (y=20,00,000 and future-equity-fund=₹46,00,000 both reconcile). A simpler single-step subtraction (54,00,000−26,00,000=28,00,000÷25=1,12,000 shares) would instead land on option (A) — flagged here so the more elaborate correct method is visible, not because the source answer is doubted; low confidence/flagged status reflects that no working was shown in the source itself, not disagreement with the letter.

MTP May 2025 Set 1 · Part I · Q5 · Marks: 2

When should RTS Ltd recognize revenue as per the Accounting Standards notified under the Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules. Would your answer be different if inspection is normally known to lead to no quality rejections?

  1. Revenue should be recognized on dispatch of components. The assessment would not change even in case where inspection is normally known to lead to no quality rejections.
  2. Revenue should be recognized on completion of inspection of components. The assessment would not change even in case where inspection is normally known to lead to no quality rejections.
  3. Revenue should be recognized on dispatch of components. The assessment would change where inspection is normally known to lead to no quality rejections.
  4. Revenue should be recognized on delivery of the component to the Railways’ stockyard. The assessment would change where inspection is normally known to lead to no quality rejections.

Answer: (B) — Railways’ inspection here is a substantive acceptance gate (physical quality-check sticker plus a separate hologram identification marking applied only after inspection), not a mere pricing formality, so revenue is recognised only on completion of inspection; a historically low/nil rejection rate does not change this, because the inspection remains a real contractual precondition of the buyer’s acceptance, unlike AS 9’s narrow exception for inspections performed solely to fix a contract price.

AS 9 (2.8)
A common error is treating "inspection rarely rejects goods" as equivalent to "inspection is a mere formality," and recognising revenue at dispatch, when Railways’ inspection here is a substantive quality-and-identification step (separate quality-check and hologram stickers) rather than a purely administrative price-fixing exercise.

Re-derived from AS 9 §2.8 (goods sold subject to installation/inspection) and the agency-relationship distinction in the ICAI study material; medium confidence since the exact "inspection is a mere formality" carve-out is a narrow exception not spelt out verbatim for this fact pattern, and the reasoning is an application by analogy rather than a directly on-point illustration found in the source material.
MTP May 2025 Set 1 · Part I · Q6 · Marks: 2

In respect of LPP, how should the revenue be recognized as per Accounting Standards?

  1. LPP should record net amount of commission earned by it.
  2. LPP should record net amount of commission earned by it and disclose the information about gross income from advertisement through media and preparation of advertisement material as well as payments to media and expenditure incurred for creation of an advertisement in the notes to accounts.
  3. LPP should record gross income from advertisement through media and preparation of advertisement material and gross amount of payments to media and expenditure incurred for creation of an advertisement.
  4. LPP may record this on net or gross basis depending on its accounting policy.

Answer: (C) — LPP is not a pure facilitating agent: it prepares/creates the advertisement material itself (a value-added service) and invoices clients for the full amount (commission plus media space payable), taking on primary billing/collection responsibility to the client while separately owing the newspaper for media space. Per AS 9’s agency-relationship principle (only a true agent's commission, not the gross proceeds, is revenue — determined by who bears the risks and rewards), LPP's role here indicates it is acting as principal, so gross income and gross media/production costs are both recorded.

AS 9 (2.3)
A common error is assuming any business described as an "agency" automatically records revenue net of commission, without checking whether it actually bears the risks/rewards and billing responsibility of a principal — LPP's own preparation of the advertisement material and full-amount invoicing are the deciding facts here.

Please guide the management of RTS Ltd as to which one of the events mentioned above (i to v) after the reporting period provide evidence of conditions that existed at the end of the reporting period?

MTP May 2025 Set 1 · Part I · Q8 · Marks: 2

Please guide regarding the accounting treatment of both the grants mentioned above in line with the requirements of Accounting Standard 12.

  1. Distribution of dividend out of grant is correct. In the second case also not recording land in the books of accounts is correct.
  2. Distribution of dividend out of grant is incorrect. In the second case, not recording land in the books of accounts is correct.
  3. Distribution of dividend out of grant is correct. In the second case, land should be recorded in the books of accounts at a nominal value.
  4. Distribution of dividend out of grant is incorrect. In the second case, land should be recorded in the books of accounts at a nominal value.

Answer: (D) — a grant received for setting up a factory (a grant related to a specific fixed asset/promoters’-contribution-style capital grant) is not free surplus available for distribution as dividend without regard to AS 12's presentation requirements, so distributing part of it as dividend is incorrect. MTP May 2025 Set 1 · Part I – General MCQ · Q14 · Marks: 2

A Machinery was giver on 3 years lease by a dealer of the machinery for equal annual lease rentals to yield 20% profit margin on cost of the machinery, which is ₹3,00,000. Economic life of the machinery is 5 years, and estimated output from the machinery in 5 years is as follows: Year I 50,000 units, Year II 60,000 units, Year III 40,000 units, Year IV 65,000 units, Year V 85,000 units. Compute Annual Lease Rent.

  1. ₹30,000
  2. ₹60,000
  3. ₹50,000
  4. ₹36,000

Answer: (B) ₹60,000. This is an operating lease (the machine returns to the dealer after the 3-year term, with 2 years of economic life remaining). Total amount the dealer targets recovering over the machine's full economic life = cost + 20% margin = ₹3,00,000 × 1.20 = ₹3,60,000, recovered progressively in proportion to how much of the machine's total lifetime output is consumed during each period of use. Output during the lease (Years I–III): 50,000+60,000+40,000 = 1,50,000 units, exactly 50% of the machine's total 5-year output (3,00,000 units). The dealer therefore targets recovering 50% of ₹3,60,000 = ₹1,80,000 during this lease, as equal annual rentals: ₹1,80,000 / 3 = ₹60,000/year.

AS 19 (5.2)
A common error is spreading the full cost-plus-margin total evenly over only the 3-year lease term (giving ₹1,20,000/year, not a listed option), missing that the dealer only needs to recover the portion of value corresponding to the output consumed during this particular lease.

MTP May 2025 Set 1 · Part II · Q6(a), alternative 2 of 2 (OR) · Marks: 4

When capitalisation of borrowing cost should cease as per Accounting Standard 16? Explain in brief.

Capitalization of borrowing costs should cease when substantially all the activities necessary to prepare the qualifying asset for its intended use or sale are complete. An asset is normally ready for its intended use or sale when its physical construction or production is complete even though routine administrative work might still continue. If minor modifications such as the decoration of a property to the user's specification, are all that are outstanding, this indicates that substantially all the activities are complete. When the construction of a qualifying asset is completed in parts and a completed part is capable of being used while construction continues for the other parts, capitalization of borrowing costs in relation to a part should cease when substantially all the activities necessary to prepare that part for its intended use or sale are complete.

AS 16 (4.11)
A common error is assuming capitalisation continues until the asset is actually put into use or sold, rather than ceasing once the asset is merely ready for its intended use, even if routine administrative work or minor finishing touches remain outstanding.
