RTP Jan 2026 · Part II · Q3

A private limited company manufacturing fancy terry towels had valued its closing inventory of finished goods at realisable value, inclusive of profit and export cash incentives. AS 2 requires inventories to be valued at the lower of historical cost and net realisable value. AS 9 permits valuing goods at net realisable value only in specific industries where performance is substantially complete prior to the transaction generating revenue — e.g. harvested agricultural crops or extracted mineral ores, where a sale is assured under forward contract/government guarantee or a ready market exists with negligible risk of failure to sell.

RTP Jan 2026 · Part II · Q12

Can interest be included in the cost of inventories?

As a general rule, AS 2 (para 12) provides that interest and other borrowing costs are usually not related to bringing inventories to their present location and condition, and are therefore usually excluded from inventory cost.

The appropriation to such a mandatory reserve should be included in net profit attributable to equity shareholders for computing Basic EPS — AB Limited's treatment is not correct.

AS 20 para 11 defines the numerator as net profit/loss for the period "attributable to equity shareholders" — after deducting preference dividends and attributable tax, but nothing else. Emphasis on "attributable to equity shareholders" (not "available for distribution") means amounts appropriated to mandatory reserves, though not distributable as dividend, remain attributable to equity shareholders in substance (the reserve still belongs to them, merely earmarked). They should therefore be included in the Basic EPS computation.

AS 20 (5.2)
Some examinees conflated "attributable to equity shareholders" with "distributable to equity shareholders," incorrectly excluding non-distributable mandatory-reserve appropriations from the EPS numerator.

RTP Jan 2026 · Part II · Q17

Swift Limited acquired patent rights to manufacture Solar Roof Top Panels for ₹600 lacs. Product life cycle estimated at 5 years; amortization decided in the ratio of future cash flows: Year 1–5: 300, 300, 300, 150, 150 (₹ lacs). After Year 3, estimated balance future life revised to 3 years, with expected cash flow after Year 5 of ₹75 lacs. Determine the amortization cost of the patent for each year per AS 26.

Years 1–3, amortized in the ratio of the originally estimated future cash flows 300:300:300:150:150 (total 1,200): each of Years 1, 2, 3 = ₹600 × 300/1,200 = ₹150 lacs/year.

Unamortized patent cost after Year 3 = 600−450 = ₹150 lacs, now amortized over the revised remaining life of 3 years (Years 4, 5, 6) in the ratio of the revised estimated future cash flows: 150:150:75 (i.e. 2:2:1). Year 4 = 150×2/5=₹60 lacs; Year 5 = ₹60 lacs; Year 6 = 150×1/5=₹30 lacs.

RTP Jan 2026 · Part II · Q18

Briefly explain the scope and forms of Joint Venture as per AS 27.

Scope: AS 27 applies to accounting for interests in joint ventures and reporting joint venture assets, liabilities, income, and expenses in the financial statements of venturers and investors, regardless of the structure/form under which the joint venture activities take place. Its consolidated-financial-statements provisions apply only when the venturer prepares and presents CFS.

Forms of Joint Venture: (i) Jointly controlled operations — no separate entity created; venturers use their own resources, raise their own funds, incur expenses, and realise sales individually, using shared assets/employees alongside their own business, with no separate books maintained for the joint venture. (ii) Jointly controlled assets — no separate legal entity, but the venturers jointly own assets used to generate economic benefit for each of them, taking up related expenses/liabilities per the contractual agreement. (iii) Jointly controlled entities — a new entity is created, pooling the venturers' resources; the entity purchases its own assets, incurs its own liabilities/expenses, and makes its own sales; the net result is shared per the contractual agreement, which also determines joint control; a separate set of books is maintained.

AS 27 (1.2)
Some examinees conflated jointly controlled assets with jointly controlled entities, missing that the defining distinction is the absence vs. presence of a separate legal entity and separate books.

RTP Jan 2026 · Part II · Q19

X Ltd. purchased a fixed asset four years ago for ₹150 lakhs, depreciating at 10% p.a. SLM. At the end of the fourth year, it revalued the asset to ₹75 lakhs, writing off the revaluation loss to P&L. On the revaluation date, market price is ₹67.50 lakhs, expected disposal costs ₹3 lakhs, and value in use is estimated at ₹60 lakhs. What is the treatment of impairment loss, given fair value for revaluation is market-value-based?

W.N. — Carrying amount at revaluation: Purchase price 150.00, less depreciation for 4 years (150/10 × 4) 60.00 = carrying value before revaluation 90.00, less downward revaluation charged to P&L (90−75) 15.00 = revalued carrying amount 75.00 lakhs.

Recoverable amount = higher of net selling price and value in use. Net selling price = market price 67.50 − disposal costs 3.00 = 64.50 lakhs. Value in use = 60 lakhs. Recoverable amount = ₹64.50 lakhs (the higher of the two).

Impairment loss = carrying amount (75.00) − recoverable amount (64.50) = ₹10.50 lakhs, recognised as an expense in the Statement of Profit and Loss immediately (since the prior downward revaluation was already charged to P&L rather than to a revaluation reserve, there is no reserve balance to absorb this further impairment against).

Some examinees used the market price directly as net selling price without deducting disposal costs, or used net selling price as recoverable amount without comparing it against value in use to find the higher of the two.

RTP Jan 2026 · Part II · Q20

An oil company has been contaminating land for several years, not cleaning up since no legislation currently requires it. At 31st March, 2025, it is virtually certain that a law requiring clean-up of contaminated land will be enacted shortly after the year end. Is provisioning presently necessary?

Per AS 29 para 29, a past event creates a present obligation only when the enterprise has no realistic alternative to settling it. Environmental contamination alone does not create an obligation to remedy it unless a law requires the existing damage to be rectified — and where a proposed law's details are not yet finalised, an obligation arises only once enactment is virtually certain.

Here, it is virtually certain that a clean-up law will be enacted. The oil company must therefore provide for the clean-up cost in the year in which the legislation's enactment becomes virtually certain (the year ended 31st March, 2025) — not deferred until the law is actually enacted.

AS 29 (2.5)
Some examinees concluded no provision was needed since the law had not yet been enacted at the balance sheet date, missing that "virtually certain" enactment is itself sufficient to trigger the obligation under AS 29.
