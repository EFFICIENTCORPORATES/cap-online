<li>Certain fundamental accounting assumptions underline the preparation and presentation of financial statements. They are usually specifically stated because their acceptance and use are not assumed.</li>

### Section B: Descriptive Questions

**Q1.**

<p>(1) As per AS-I disclosure of accounting policies is not a remedy for wrong or inappropriate treatment in accounting. In the given case the financial statement does not give a true and fair view as they are not in compliance with AS-2.</p>
<p>(2) Considering the substance over form as per AS-I, documentation and legal formalities represent the form of the transaction, although the legal title has not been transferred, the economic reality and substance are that the rights and beneficial interest in the Office Building have been transferred. Therefore, recording of acquisition/disposal (by the transferee and transferor respectively) would in substance represent the transaction entered into.</p>
<p>(3) Accrual is a fundamental accounting assumption. If it is not followed by the company, the facts should be disclosed under AS-I. Hence the company should disclose the fact that the cash basis of accounting has been followed in the notes on accounts.</p>
<p>(4) The practice followed by the company is not correct. It should be disclosed as part of financial statements (The director's report is not part of financial statements).</p>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A number of examinees, when asked to comment on the AS 1 disclosure scenarios, incorrectly concluded that disclosing a departure from a specific standard such as AS 2 in the notes cures the non-compliance and preserves a true-and-fair view, not recognising that AS 1 disclosure is a transparency requirement and never a substitute for correct accounting treatment. Some also failed to identify that accounting policy disclosure belongs in the financial statements themselves, not the Directors' Report.

**Q2.**

<ol type="i">
<li><strong>False;</strong> As per AS 1 “Disclosure of Accounting Policies”, certain fundamental accounting assumptions underlie the preparation and presentation of financial statements. They are usually not specifically stated because their acceptance and use are assumed. Disclosure is necessary if they are not followed.</li>
<li><strong>False;</strong> As per AS 1, if the fundamental accounting assumptions, viz. Going Concern, Consistency and Accrual are followed in financial statements, specific disclosure is not required. If a fundamental accounting assumption is not followed, the fact should be disclosed.</li>
<li><strong>True;</strong> To ensure proper understanding of financial statements, it is necessary that all significant accounting policies adopted in the preparation and presentation of financial statements should be disclosed. The disclosure of the significant accounting policies as such should form part of the financial statements and they should be disclosed at one place.</li>
<li><strong>False;</strong> Any change in the accounting policies which has a material effect in the current period or which is reasonably expected to have a material effect in later periods should be disclosed. Where such amount is not ascertainable, wholly or in part, the fact should be indicated.</li>
<li><strong>True;</strong> As per AS 1, there is no single list of accounting policies which are applicable to all circumstances. The differing circumstances in which enterprises operate in a situation of diverse and complex economic activity make alternative accounting principles and methods of applying those principles acceptable.</li>
</ol>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A common error is inverting statements (i) and (ii), reasoning that fundamental accounting assumptions must be specifically stated precisely because they are so foundational, when AS 1's actual rule is the opposite - they are presumed and go unstated unless departed from, at which point disclosure becomes mandatory.

**Q3.**

<div>
<p>The decision of making provision for non-moving inventories on the basis of technical evaluation does not amount to change in accounting policy. Accounting policy of a company may require that provision for non-moving inventories should be made but the basis for making provision will not constitute accounting policy. The method of estimating the amount of provision may be changed in case a more prudent estimate can be made.</p>
<p>In the given case, considering the total value of inventory, the change in the amount of required provision of non-moving inventory from ₹4 lakhs to ₹3 lakhs is also not material. The disclosure can be made for such change in the following lines by way of notes to the accounts in the annual accounts of HIL Ltd. for the year 2021-22 in the following manner:</p>
<p><em>"The company has provided for non-moving inventories on the basis of technical evaluation unlike preceding years. Had the same method been followed as in the previous year, the profit for the year and the value of net assets at the end of the year would have been lower by ₹1 lakh."</em></p>
</div>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A common error is treating a change in the basis of estimating a provision (technical evaluation vs. the historical no-issues test) as a change in accounting policy requiring retrospective restatement, rather than recognising it as a change in accounting estimate needing only prospective disclosure.

**Q6.**

<p>(i) In this case, accountant of company created a provision for damages of probability of losing a suit by a charge against profits. Unless the probability of losing the suit is more than probability of not losing it, there should not be any creation of provision for such probable losses. So there is no need to charge such loss against profit and disclosing the same in financial statements.</p>
<p>(ii) Repairs and maintenance are revenue expenditure and should not be added to the value of assets, as these expenses do not increase the capacity of asset. Hence such expenses should be charged to profit &amp; loss statement. Further the chief accountant also disclosed its policy of adding repairs to value of assets by way of notes to accounts. As per AS 1 disclosure is not a method to correct the wrong treatments. So the contention of chief accountant is wrong.</p>
<p>(iii) Accrual is one of the Fundamental accounting assumptions. If fundamental accounting assumptions are followed properly then no specific disclosure is required. Disclosure is required only when there is deviation and the company is not following fundamental accounting assumptions. So the company need not disclose this in financial statements.</p>
<p>(iv) As per AS 1, any change in the accounting policies which has a material effect in the current period or which is reasonably expected to have a material effect in later periods should be disclosed. Accordingly, the notes on accounts should properly disclose the change and its effect.</p>
<p><em>Note: So far, the company has been providing 2% of sales for meeting after sales expenses during the warranty period. Now the company has improved the quality of its products with better technology and has been observing that actual expenses are very less than the provision, Hence, the company has decided not to make provision for such expenses but to account for the same as and when expenses are incurred. Due to this change, the profit for the year is increased by ₹1 crore than would have been the case if the old policy were to continue.</em></p>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** Several examinees agreed with the accountant's policy of capitalising repairs and maintenance merely because it had been disclosed as an accounting policy, not recognising that disclosure under AS 1 cannot cure a treatment that is otherwise incorrect.

**Q8.**

<p><strong>Effect:</strong> closing inventory falls from ₹5,00,000 to ₹4,60,000, a reduction of ₹40,000; profit for the year is therefore also reduced by ₹40,000.</p><p><strong>Disclosure:</strong> as per AS 1, any change in accounting policy which has a material effect in the current period, or is reasonably expected to have a material effect in a later period, should be disclosed. Where the change has a material effect in the current period, the amount by which any item in the financial statements is affected should also be disclosed to the extent ascertainable; where it is not ascertainable wholly or in part, that fact should be indicated. A bare statement that a policy has changed is of little use to a reader, so the effect should be given. A suitable note: <em>'The company values its inventory at lower of cost or net realisable value. In the present year the company has changed to the weighted average method, which better reflects the consumption pattern of inventory, for ascertaining inventory costs from the earlier practice of using FIFO. The change in policy has reduced profit for the year and value of inventory as at the year end by ₹40,000.'</em></p><p><strong>No material effect in the current period:</strong> if the change is reasonably expected to have a material effect in future periods, it must still be disclosed in the current period, even though it has no material effect now. This ensures that all important changes in accounting policies are actually disclosed.</p>

**Q9.**

<p><strong>(a) Prudence.</strong> Because of uncertainty about future events, profits are not anticipated but losses are provided for as a matter of conservatism. Provision should be made for all known liabilities and losses even though the amount cannot be determined with certainty and is only a best estimate on the information available. Exercising prudence ensures that (i) profits are not overstated, (ii) losses are not understated, (iii) assets are not overstated and (iv) liabilities are not understated. The common example is valuing inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Prudence does not permit creation of hidden reserves by understating profits and assets or overstating liabilities and losses; for instance, for a pending damages suit no provision is charged to profit unless the probability of losing the suit is greater than the probability of not losing it.</p><p><strong>(b) Materiality.</strong> Financial statements should disclose all material items, i.e. items the knowledge of which might influence the decisions of the user. Materiality is not always a matter of relative size: for example, a small amount lost through fraud by certain employees may indicate a serious flaw in internal control that needs immediate attention. In certain cases quantitative limits are specified. Under Schedule III, a company should disclose by way of notes additional information on any item of income or expenditure which exceeds 1% of the revenue from operations or ₹1,00,000, whichever is higher; and should disclose in the Notes to Accounts the shares held by each shareholder holding more than 5% of the shares, specifying the number of shares held.</p>
