**Q1.**

<div>
<p>Accounting Standards standardize diverse accounting policies with a view to eliminate the non-comparability of financial statements and improve the reliability of financial statements. Accounting Standards provide a set of standard accounting policies, valuation norms and disclosure requirements. Accounting standards aim at improving the quality of financial reporting by promoting comparability, consistency and transparency, in the interests of users of financial statements.</p>
<p>The following are the benefits of Accounting Standards:</p>
<p>(i) <strong>Standardization of alternative accounting treatments:</strong> Accounting Standards reduce to a reasonable extent confusing variations in the accounting treatment followed for the purpose of preparation of financial statements.</p>
<p>(ii) <strong>Requirements for additional disclosures:</strong> There are certain areas where important information is not statutorily required to be disclosed. Standards may call for disclosure beyond that required by law.</p>
<p>(iii) <strong>Comparability of financial statements:</strong> The application of accounting standards would facilitate comparison of financial statements of different companies situated in India and facilitate comparison, to a limited extent, of financial statements of companies situated in different parts of the world. However, it should be noted in this respect that differences in the institutions, traditions and legal systems from one country to another give rise to differences in Accounting Standards adopted in different countries.</p>
</div>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A common error is listing the benefits of Accounting Standards as a generic definition of what AS is, rather than specifically explaining how they reduce comparability problems, mandate additional disclosure, and aid cross-company/cross-border comparison.

**Q2.**

<div>
<p>Accounting Standards deal with the issues of (i) Recognition of events and transactions in the financial statements, (ii) Measurement of these transactions and events, (iii) Presentation of these transactions and events in the financial statements in a manner that is meaningful and understandable to the reader, and (iv) Disclosure requirements.</p>
</div>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A common error is listing only two or three of the four issues (recognition, measurement, presentation, disclosure), or substituting a generic description of "what Accounting Standards are" for this specific four-part enumeration the question asks for.

**Q3.**

<p>Accounting Standards are the written policy documents issued by Government relating to various aspects of measurement, treatment, presentation and disclosure of accounting transactions and events.</p>
<p><strong>Objectives of Accounting Standards:</strong> (a) Accounting Standards harmonize the diverse accounting policies and practices followed by different companies in India. (b) Accounting Standards facilitate the preparation of financial statements and make them comparable. (c) Accounting Standards give a sense of faith and reliability to the users.</p>
<p><strong>Advantages of setting Accounting Standards:</strong> (a) Accounting Standards make the financial statements of different companies comparable, which helps investors in decision making. (b) Accounting Standards prevent any misleading accounting treatment. (c) Accounting Standards prevent manipulation of data by the management.</p>

> **Examiner's Comment:** A few examinees could not clearly explain objectives of "Accounting Standards" and advantages of setting the Accounting Standards.

**Q4.**

<div>
<p>Certain changes have been made in Ind AS considering the economic environment of the country, which is different as compared to the economic environment presumed to be in existence by IFRS. These differences are due to differences in economic conditions prevailing in India. These differences which are in deviation to the accounting principles and practices stated in IFRS, are commonly known as 'Carve-outs'. Additional guidance given in Ind AS over and above what is given in IFRS, is termed as 'Carve in'.</p>
</div>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A common error is treating every difference between Ind AS and IFRS as a "carve-out", without recognising that removal of an option available under IFRS is explicitly not treated as a carve-out under the Ind AS framework.

**Q5.**

<p>A few key aspects which required the need for convergence are as under:</p>
<p>1. Raising funds from international markets: Each country has its own set of rules and regulations for accounting and financial reporting. Therefore, when an enterprise decides to raise capital from markets other than the country in which it is located, the rules and regulations of that other country will apply, requiring the enterprise to understand the differences between the rules governing financial reporting in the foreign country as compared to its own.</p>
<p>2. Comparability of Financial Statements: International analysts and investors would like to compare financial statements based on similar ASs, leading to a growing need for an internationally accepted set of ASs for cross-border filings, raising investor confidence generally.</p>
<p>3. Uniformity, Comparability, Transparency etc.: A strong need was felt to bring about uniformity, rationalisation, comparability, transparency and adaptability in financial statements. A multiplicity of AS systems worldwide produces different reported numbers for the same events, creating confusion and encouraging error or fraud. The cure is a single set of global standards, of the highest quality, set in the public interest.</p>
<p>4. Global Investment: Convergence contributes to the free flow of global investment, improves investors' ability to compare investments globally (lowering risk of errors of judgment), facilitates accounting/reporting for companies with global operations, eliminates costly requirements like reinstatement of financial statements, creates a new standard of accountability and transparency, and reduces operational challenges for accounting firms.</p>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** Several examinees listed only one or two convergence drivers instead of the full range (fund-raising, comparability, uniformity, global investment), losing marks for an incomplete answer to a theory question that explicitly asks for "key aspects" in the plural.

**Q6.**

<p>The ASB, constituted by the ICAI in 1977, includes representatives of industry, associations of industry (ASSOCHAM, CII, FICCI), regulators, academicians and government departments. Though constituted by the Council of the ICAI, it is independent in formulating accounting standards. The steps are:</p><ol><li><strong>Identification of area:</strong> the ASB identifies broad areas for which a standard is to be formulated.</li><li><strong>Constitution of study groups:</strong> study groups prepare preliminary drafts (objective and scope, definitions, recognition and measurement principles, presentation and disclosure requirements); the ASB considers and, if needed, revises them.</li><li><strong>Preparation of draft and its circulation:</strong> the revised draft is circulated to the Council members and specified outside bodies such as MCA, SEBI, C&amp;AG, CBDT and SCOPE for comments.</li><li><strong>Ascertainment of views:</strong> meetings are held with representatives of the outside bodies to ascertain their views on the draft.</li><li><strong>Finalisation of exposure draft (E.D.):</strong> the exposure draft is finalised and issued inviting public comments.</li><li><strong>Comments on the exposure draft:</strong> the ASB considers the comments and finalises the draft standard for submission to the Council.</li><li><strong>Modification of the draft:</strong> the Council considers the final draft and, if necessary, modifies it in consultation with the ASB.</li><li><strong>Issue of the AS:</strong> the standard is issued by the ICAI (for non-corporate entities).</li></ol><p><strong>Role of NFRA and MCA:</strong> for corporate entities, the standards are issued by the MCA in consultation with the NFRA. Under Section 133 of the Companies Act, 2013, the Central Government may prescribe standards of accounting as recommended by the ICAI, in consultation with and after examination of the recommendations of the NFRA. NFRA recommends the standards to the MCA, and the MCA specifies the standards applicable to companies.</p>

**Q7.**

<p><strong>(a)</strong> <em>Adoption:</em> the country sets a specific timetable when specific entities are required to use IFRS as issued by the IASB. <em>Convergence:</em> the country develops high-quality, compatible accounting standards and aligns its standards with those of other standard setters with a certain rate of compromise, by adopting the requirements of the standards either fully or partially. Ind AS are almost similar to IFRS but with a few carve-outs to suit the Indian environment.</p><p><strong>(b)</strong> India converged after a detailed analysis of the IFRS requirements and extensive discussion with stakeholders; Ind AS are kept as close to the corresponding IAS/IFRS as possible, with departures only where absolutely essential, on account of:</p><ul><li><strong>Terminology differences</strong> - to align with law, e.g. 'statement of profit and loss' instead of 'statement of comprehensive income' and 'balance sheet' instead of 'statement of financial position';</li><li><strong>Removal of options</strong> in accounting principles and practices, for consistency and comparability (these do not result in carve-outs);</li><li><strong>Differences in the economic environment</strong> of India compared with that assumed by IFRS.</li></ul><p>Departures from the accounting principles and practices in IFRS arising from differences in economic conditions are known as <strong>carve-outs</strong>. Additional guidance given in Ind AS over and above IFRS is a <strong>carve-in</strong>.</p><p><strong>(c)</strong> Benefits of convergence: (i) improves investor confidence across the world through transparency and comparability; (ii) improves inter-unit, inter-firm and inter-industry comparison; (iii) group consolidation is easier when all group companies use the same standard irrespective of location; (iv) financial statements are acceptable to stock exchanges across the globe, facilitating listing of Indian companies on international exchanges.</p>

**Q8.**

<p><strong>(a)</strong> There are effectively <strong>27</strong> Accounting Standards at present. AS 6 on 'Depreciation Accounting' was withdrawn on the revision of AS 10 'Property, Plant and Equipment' (earlier AS 10 was 'Accounting for Fixed Assets'), and AS 8 on 'Accounting for Research and Development' was withdrawn consequent to the issuance of AS 26 'Intangible Assets'. The Companies Act had earlier notified 28 standards; in 2016 the MCA withdrew AS 6, leaving 27 notified standards under the Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules, 2021.</p><p><strong>(b)</strong> The standards are developed by the ASB of the ICAI. The Institute is not a legislative body and can enforce compliance with its standards only by its members. The standards cannot override laws and local regulations; where there is conflict, the statute prevails. They are nevertheless made mandatory from the dates specified, and are generally applicable to all enterprises subject to certain exceptions. The implication of mandatory status depends on whether the statute governing the enterprise requires compliance with them; for companies, the MCA has notified them under the Companies Act, and the Rules of 2021 apply to accounting periods beginning on or after 1st April, 2021.</p><p><strong>(c)</strong> AS 2, AS 4, AS 10, AS 13, AS 14, AS 21 and AS 29 were revised to make them in line with the corresponding Ind AS to the extent possible.</p>
