*Case Facts (Case CS-1):*

<p>Mars Ltd. is a manufacturing enterprise which is starting a new manufacturing plant at X Village. It has

    Plant @12% per annum on April 1, 2023. Director finance of the Company incurred travel and meeting expenses

    activities of the plant were interrupted as the local people alongwith the activists have raised issues relating

    standstill for 3 months. With the help of Government and NGOs, the agitation was over by February 28, 2024 and

### Section A — MCQ Answer Key & Explanations

**1.** Correct Option: **(B)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (B) ₹2,00,000 — full-year borrowing
  cost = ₹25,00,000 × 12% = ₹3,00,000. AS 16 requires capitalisation to be suspended during an
  extended period in which active development is interrupted (the environmental-agitation halt is not a routine,
  necessary part of the construction process, so it is a genuine suspension, not merely a temporary delay). Treating
  the suspension as running from 1 November, 2023 to 28 February, 2024 (4 months, per the resumption date actually
  given), the eligible capitalisation period is the remaining 8 months (April–October, 7 months, plus March,
  1 month): ₹3,00,000 × 8/12 = <strong>₹2,00,000</strong>.</p>

**2.** Correct Option: **(D)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (D) ₹1,64,000 — borrowing cost on the ₹28,00,000 specific loan for the instructed 9-month capitalisation period: ₹28,00,000 × 9% × 9/12 = ₹1,89,000, less the ₹25,000 investment income earned on the temporary investment of the unutilised loan proceeds (netted per AS 16) = ₹1,64,000.</p>

**3.** Correct Option: **(B)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (B) ₹2,00,000 — loan ₹25,00,000 @12% for the full year would give ₹3,00,000, but AS 16 requires suspension of capitalisation during the extended interruption caused by the environmental agitation (an externally-imposed halt, not a necessary technical part of construction). Using the dates actually given (interruption 1 Nov 2023 – 28 Feb 2024 = 4 months), active construction = 8 months: ₹25,00,000 × 12% × 8/12 = ₹2,00,000.</p>

**4.** Correct Option: **(C)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (c) ₹63 Lakhs = ₹70 Lakhs interest − ₹7 Lakhs temporary-investment income.</p>

**5.** Correct Option: **(C)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (c) ₹3.5 Lakhs — the completed 100 km stretch is 5% of the 2000 km pipeline; capitalisation ceases for that part (5% × ₹70 Lakhs) while the rest of the project continues.</p>

### Section B — Descriptive Solutions

**Q1.**

<p>Para 10 of AS 16 'Borrowing Costs' states that to the extent the funds are borrowed specifically for the purpose of obtaining a qualifying asset, the amount of borrowing costs eligible for capitalization on that asset should be determined as the actual borrowing costs incurred on that borrowing during the period less any income on the temporary investment of those borrowings. The capitalization rate should be the weighted average of the borrowing costs applicable to the borrowings of the enterprise that are outstanding during the period, other than borrowings made specifically for the purpose of obtaining a qualifying asset. Hence, in the above case, treatment of accountant of Vital Ltd. is incorrect. The amount of borrowing costs capitalized for the financial year 2021-22 should be calculated as follows:</p>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Particulars</th><th>₹</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Actual interest for 2021-22 (10% of ₹150 crores)</td><td>15.00 crores</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less: Income on temporary investment from specific borrowings</td><td>(1.50 crores)</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Borrowing costs to be capitalized during year 2021-22</strong></td><td><strong>13.50 crores</strong></td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

> **Author's Note (Synthesized — not ICAI-sourced):** A common error is applying the enterprise's weighted average cost of capital (or the general-borrowings capitalisation rate) to a loan that was specifically raised for the qualifying asset, instead of using the actual interest rate on that specific borrowing net of any temporary-investment income earned on the surplus funds.

**Q2.**

<p>As per AS 16 ‘Borrowing Costs’, a qualifying asset is one that necessarily takes a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended use or sale. What constitutes a substantial period of time primarily depends on the facts and circumstances of each case; ordinarily a period of twelve months is considered substantial unless a shorter or longer period can be justified. In estimating the period, the time an asset takes, technologically and commercially, to get ready for its intended use or sale is considered — there is a rebuttable presumption that 12 months constitutes a substantial period.</p>
<p>Under circumstances where the construction period has reduced drastically due to technical innovation, the 12-month period should be looked at as a benchmark, not a conclusive yardstick. An enterprise that completes the asset in 8 months should not be penalised for its efficiency by being denied interest capitalisation. The substantial-period criterion exists to ensure enterprises do not spend disproportionate time and effort capturing immaterial interest cost for capitalisation purposes.</p>
<p><strong>Conclusion:</strong> since the factory was constructed in 8 months (a genuinely justified, technically-innovation-driven reduction), it is still considered a qualifying asset, and the interest on borrowings for the same shall be capitalised, although it took less than 12 months for the asset to get ready for use.</p>

> **Author's Note (Synthesized — not ICAI-sourced):** A common error here is treating the 12-month benchmark as a rigid, non-rebuttable rule and denying capitalisation outright once construction takes under 12 months, without weighing the facts-and-circumstances test AS 16 actually applies.
