**Q1.**

<p>The Framework for Recognition and Presentation of Financial statements recognizes four alternative measurement bases for the purpose of determining the value at which an element can be recognized in the balance sheet or statement of profit and loss. These bases are: (i) Historical Cost; (ii) Current cost (iii) Realizable (Settlement) Value and (iv) Present Value.</p>
<p>A brief explanation of each measurement basis is as follows:</p>
<ol type="1">
<li><strong>Historical Cost:</strong> Historical cost means acquisition price. According to this, assets are recorded at an amount of cash or cash equivalent paid or the fair value of the asset at the time of acquisition. Liabilities are generally recorded at the amount of proceeds received in exchange for the obligation.</li>
<li><strong>Current Cost:</strong> Current cost gives an alternative measurement basis. Assets are carried out at the amount of cash or cash equivalent that would have to be paid if the same or an equivalent asset was acquired currently. Liabilities are carried at the undiscounted amount of cash or cash equivalents that would be required to settle the obligation currently.</li>
<li><strong>Realisable (Settlement) Value:</strong> As per realizable value, assets are carried at the amount of cash or cash equivalents that could currently be obtained by selling the assets in an orderly disposal. Liabilities are carried at their settlement values; i.e. the undiscounted amount of cash or cash equivalents paid to satisfy the liabilities in the normal course of business.</li>
<li><strong>Present Value:</strong> Under present value convention, assets are carried at present value of future net cash flows generated by the concerned assets in the normal course of business. Liabilities under this convention are carried at present value of future net cash flows that are expected to be required to settle the liability in the normal course of business.</li>
</ol>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A common error is confusing Current Cost with Realisable Value - both involve a “current” amount, but Current Cost is the cost to acquire an equivalent asset today while Realisable Value is the amount obtainable by selling the existing asset today, a distinction the Framework treats as two genuinely separate bases.

**Q3.**

<p>In the given case, the specific price index applicable to the product is 125 (25/20 × 100).</p>
<p>Current cost of opening stock = (₹1,20,000/100) × 125 Or 6,000 units × ₹25 = ₹1,50,000</p>
<p>Current cost of closing cash = ₹1,20,000 (₹1,80,000 − ₹60,000)</p>
<p>Opening equity at closing current costs = ₹1,50,000</p>
<p>Closing equity at closing current costs = ₹1,20,000</p>
<p>Retained Profit = ₹1,20,000 − ₹1,50,000 = (−) ₹30,000</p>
<p>The negative retained profit indicates that the trader has failed to maintain his capital. The available fund of ₹1,20,000 is not sufficient to buy 6,000 units again at increased price of ₹25 per unit. The drawings should have been restricted to ₹30,000 (₹60,000 − ₹30,000).</p>
<p>If the trader had not withdrawn any amount, then the answer would have been as below:</p>
<p>Current cost of opening stock = ₹1,80,000</p>
<p>Opening equity at closing current costs = ₹1,50,000</p>
<p>Retained Profit = ₹1,80,000 − ₹1,50,000 = ₹30,000</p>
<p>If the trader had not withdrawn any amount, then the retained profit would have been ₹30,000.</p>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A common error is computing retained profit as historical sale proceeds less historical cost of goods sold (a financial-capital-maintenance answer) instead of restating the opening stock to its current replacement cost and comparing that against the closing cash balance, which is what the physical-capital-maintenance concept specifically requires.

**Q4.**

<p>(i) (1) Users of financial statements: Investors, Employees, Lenders, Suppliers/Creditors, Customers, Government &amp; Public.<br/>
    (2) Qualitative Characteristics of Financial Statements: Understandability, Relevance, Comparability, Reliability &amp; Faithful Representation.<br/>
    (3) Elements of Financial Statements: Asset, Liability, Equity, Income/Gain and Expense/Loss.</p>
<p>(ii) Fundamental Accounting Assumptions: Accrual, Going Concern and Consistency.</p>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A common error is confusing the four qualitative characteristics (Understandability, Relevance, Reliability, Comparability) with the three fundamental accounting assumptions (Going Concern, Accrual, Consistency), or listing "Materiality"/"Prudence" as standalone qualitative characteristics rather than as components nested within Relevance and Reliability respectively.

**Q7.**

<div>
<p>The qualitative characteristics are attributes that improve the usefulness of information provided in financial statements. Understandability; Relevance; Reliability; Comparability are the qualitative characteristics of financial statements.</p>
<p><strong>Understandability</strong> - Information presented in financial statements should be readily understandable by the users with reasonable knowledge of business and economic activities.</p>
<p><strong>Relevance</strong> - Financial statements should contain relevant information only. Information, which is likely to influence the economic decisions of the users is called relevant.</p>
<p><strong>Reliability</strong> - Information must be reliable; that is to say, they must be free from material error and bias.</p>
<p><strong>Comparability</strong> - Financial statements should provide both inter-firm and intra-firm comparison.</p>
</div>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A common error is naming only two or three of the four characteristics, or confusing "Reliability" (freedom from material error/bias) with "Relevance" (capacity to influence economic decisions) - the two are frequently swapped in a rushed answer.

**Q8.**

<div>
<ol>
<li><strong>Assets:</strong> a resource controlled by the enterprise from past events, expected to bring future economic benefits (e.g. plant, patents, receivables).</li>
<li><strong>Liabilities:</strong> a present obligation arising from past events, settlement of which is expected to result in an outflow of economic benefits (e.g. borrowings, provisions).</li>
<li><strong>Equity:</strong> the residual interest in assets after deducting all liabilities - owners' claim (capital, reserves), distinct from liabilities (claims of parties other than owners); changes through contributions/distributions or income/expenses.</li>
<li><strong>Income/Gain:</strong> increases in economic benefits through inflow of assets or reduction of liabilities, increasing equity (includes revenue and gains).</li>
<li><strong>Expenses/Loss:</strong> decreases in economic benefits through outflows/depletion of assets or incurrence of liabilities, decreasing equity (other than distributions to owners) - expenses arise in the ordinary course (e.g. wages); losses may or may not (e.g. loss on PPE disposal), and are shown separately to aid performance assessment.</li>
</ol>
</div>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A direct theory recall question; common gaps include omitting the equity definition's distinction from liabilities, or failing to note that losses (unlike ordinary expenses) may fall outside the enterprise's normal activities.

**Q10.**

<div>
<p>Yes, one of the characteristics of financial statements is neutrality. To be reliable, the information contained in financial statement must be neutral, that is free from bias. Financial Statements are not neutral if by the selection or presentation of information, the focus of analysis could shift from one area of business to another thereby arriving at a totally different conclusion on the business results.</p>
</div>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A common error is confusing neutrality with conservatism/prudence, treating a deliberately cautious understatement of results as still "neutral" when it is in fact a form of bias in the opposite direction.
