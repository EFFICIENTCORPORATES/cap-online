### Section A: Multiple Choice Questions

**1.** Correct Answer: **(D)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (D) The entity has to recognize deferred tax liability/asset arising out of timing difference. There are no conditions which are required to evaluated for their recognition.</p>

**2.** Correct Answer: **(A)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (A) Both are correct.</p>

**3.** Correct Answer: **(D)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (D) Deferred Tax Liability = ₹ 1,35,000.</p>

**4.** Correct Answer: **(C)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (C) Either (a) or (b)</p>

**5.** Correct Answer: **(B)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (ii) ₹4,50,000 (deferred tax liability) - tax depreciation (₹15,00,000) exceeds book depreciation (₹6,00,000) by ₹9,00,000, a timing difference that will reverse in later years as book depreciation catches up, so it originates a deferred tax liability: ₹9,00,000 × 50% = ₹4,50,000.</p>

**6.** Correct Answer: **(C)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (C) ₹1,23,600. Opening DTA ₹1,20,000, <strong>less</strong> reversal of last year's PF-contribution-disallowance DTA (₹82,000 × 20% = ₹16,400, since the amount disallowed in 2022-23 is now allowed in 2023-24), <strong>plus</strong> a new DTA on the unamortized preliminary expenses still to be claimed for tax (₹1,00,000 × 20% = ₹20,000). The interest accrued in books but paid on 15-05-2024 creates <strong>no</strong> timing difference: since it was paid before the due date of filing the return of income, Section 43B's proviso allows it as a deduction in the year of accrual itself, so no DTA arises on this item. Net: ₹1,20,000 − ₹16,400 + ₹20,000 = ₹1,23,600.</p>

**7.** Correct Answer: **(A)**

<p><strong>Answer:</strong> (A) DTL ₹1,71,000 - net timing difference: depreciation (₹10,00,000 tax vs ₹4,00,000 book, a ₹6,00,000 taxable timing difference) less unamortised preliminary expenses (₹30,000 deductible timing difference); the GST penalty is not a timing difference (a permanent difference, not deductible for tax at all) and is excluded. Net ₹5,70,000 × 30% = ₹1,71,000 DTL.</p>

**Q3.**

<p>As per AS 22, ‘Accounting for Taxes on Income’, deferred tax in respect of timing differences which originate during the tax holiday period and reverse during the tax holiday period, should not be recognised to the extent deduction from the total income of an enterprise is allowed during the tax holiday period as per the provisions of sections 10A and 10B of the Income- tax Act. Deferred tax in respect of timing differences which originate during the tax holiday period but reverse after the tax holiday period should be recognised in the year in which the timing differences originate. However, recognition of deferred tax assets should be subject to the consideration of prudence. For this purpose, the timing differences which originate first should be considered to reverse first. Out of 200 lakhs timing difference due to depreciation, difference amounting 80 lakhs (10 lakhs x 8 years) will reverse in the tax holiday period and therefore, should not be recognised. However, for 120 lakhs (200 lakhs - ₹ 80 lakhs), deferred tax liability will be recognised for 48 lakhs (40% of 120 lakhs) in first year. In the second year, the entire amount of timing difference of ₹ 400 lakhs will reverse only after tax holiday period and hence, will be recognised in full. Deferred tax liability amounting 160 lakhs (40% of 400 lakhs) will be created by charging it to profit and loss account and the total balance of deferred tax liability account at the end of second year will be 208 lakhs (48 lakhs + 160 lakhs).</p>

**Q5.**

<div>
<p>Matching of taxes against revenue for a period poses special problems arising from the fact that in a number of cases, taxable income may be different from the accounting income. The divergence between taxable income and accounting income arises due to two main reasons: firstly, there are differences between items of revenue and expenses as appearing in the statement of profit and loss and the items which are considered as revenue, expenses or deductions for tax purposes, known as <strong>Permanent Difference</strong>. Secondly, there are differences between the amount in respect of a particular item of revenue or expense as recognised in the statement of profit and loss and the corresponding amount which is recognised for the computation of taxable income, known as <strong>Timing Difference</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Permanent differences</strong> are the differences between taxable income and accounting income which arise in one accounting period and do not reverse subsequently. For example, an income exempt from tax or an expense that is not allowable as a deduction for tax purposes.</p>
<p><strong>Timing differences</strong> are those differences between taxable income and accounting income which arise in one accounting period and are capable of reversal in one or more subsequent periods. For e.g., Depreciation, Bonus, etc.</p>
</div>

> **Common Mistake to Avoid:** A common error here is treating any book-tax gap as a timing difference by default; only differences capable of reversing in a future period (like depreciation) are timing differences, while items that never reverse (like a permanently tax-exempt income) are permanent differences and never generate deferred tax.
