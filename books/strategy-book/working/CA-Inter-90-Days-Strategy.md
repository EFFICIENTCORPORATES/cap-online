### DAY 1–4: THE FOUNDATION WORK

Before revision strategies, two things must be settled. Give them the first
4 days. Don't skip this. An unverified boundary and a disorganised material
setup will cost you far more than 4 days over the next 90.

---

### Strategy 1 — Fix and Tighten Your Boundary [ALL]

If you haven't fixed your boundary, do it now — next 4 days, non-negotiable.
If you have one already — review it and tighten it. Remove anything you
genuinely cannot revise before the exam.

**What goes in the boundary:**
- Minimum: every chapter from Study Material asked even once in the last
  5 attempts.
- Maximum: all Study Material + last 5 attempts' papers.

**The one rule once it's fixed:** anything inside gets a minimum of 3 revisions.
No skipping something because it's hard. In or out — decide. Once it's in,
it gets done.

> **The 3-question filter — before you add anything new:**
> ```
> 1. Can I complete this before the exam?
> 2. Can I revise this before the exam?
> 3. Can I revise this MULTIPLE times before the exam?
> ```
> If any answer is No — don't add it. Smaller, fully-revised boundary beats
> bigger, half-covered boundary every single time.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> "My personal rule: if I can't revise something in the last 24 hours before the
> exam, it doesn't belong in my boundary. Whatever you promise yourself you'll
> cover — you must be able to revise all of it the day before the exam. If you
> can't, your boundary is too big."

---

### Strategy 2 — Physical vs Digital: Know What Lives Where [ALL]

Most students never make this decision consciously — and pay for it in a panic
at the worst possible moment. Draw the line now, once, and never think about it
again.

**PHYSICAL — must be physical, no exceptions:**
- Layer 1 notebooks (class notes, numbered P01-001 etc.)
- Layer 2 revision notebook (one per subject)
- Layer 3 bound book (your exam-centre book — built in Next 45 Days)
- Error register (if kept in notebooks)
- Vision Board + Dream Marksheet + Boundary page

**DIGITAL — drive / phone:**
- RTPs, MTPs, PYQs (downloaded PDFs)
- Audio revision notes
- AI conversations / MCQ HTML files
- Scanned copies of important pages (backup only, never primary)

> **WARNING:**
> Layer 3 is NEVER digital as primary. If your phone dies at 7 AM on exam day,
> your physical Layer 3 notebook doesn't care. It is still there. Always physical.
> No exceptions.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> Anything digital must live on cloud — not just on your phone. Google Drive
> gives you 15 GB free. That is more than enough for every PDF, audio note, and
> document in your boundary. Phone lost or broken — log in from any device and
> everything is still there.
>
> Set up one Gmail, open Google Drive, create subject-wise folders. Inside each
> subject folder, three subfolders:
> ```
> 📁 Advanced Accounts (P01)
>   📁 Layer 1 — Anchor Material
>   📁 Layer 2 — Revision Notes
>   📁 Layer 3 — Exam Day Prep
> ```
> File names that describe what's inside. 30 minutes once. Saves hours later.
>
> For heavy files — revision videos you've recorded — upload to YouTube as
> **Unlisted** or **Private**. Off your phone storage, accessible anywhere.

### Strategy 3 — Understand the Three Layers [ALL]

Your revision material works in three layers. Each has a different job.
Get this clear before you start — or you'll build the wrong layer at the
wrong time.

**LAYER 1 — Raw Working Material** (built during classes)
Your anchor book + classroom notes + homework. Thick, messy, complete.
This is your source of truth. Once Layer 2 exists, you'll rarely open this
except to cross-reference.

**LAYER 2 — Revision Notes** (built now, in this phase)
One clean notebook per subject. Compressed concept notes, your error register,
exam tricks, mnemonics, important-question summaries. Where a concept is already
clear in Layer 1, Layer 2 just points to it ("see P01-002, p.14") instead of
rewriting it. This is what you revise from.

**LAYER 3 — High-Level Revision Flow** (built in Next 45 Days)
The glue. The thin top layer that lets you navigate a whole subject fast.
10 minutes for a practical subject, 15–20 for theory/law. This is what you
carry to the exam centre.

```
Layer 1  → built in classes    → raw, complete, messy
Layer 2  → built now           → compressed, clean, references Layer 1
Layer 3  → built in 45 days    → skeleton, fast, carried to exam
```

Right now your only job is to build Layer 2 and the Three Sanjeevani Bootis.
Layer 3 comes in the next phase.

---

### THE THREE SANJEEVANI BOOTIS

These three things must exist for every chapter in your boundary.
Every chapter. Every subject. That is the target for this phase.

They are not optional extras. They are the difference between revision that
lands and revision that slides off.

---

### Strategy 4 — Sanjeevani Booti 1: The Golden Nuggets Register [ALL]

Every time you sit to watch a revision video — One Shot, Exam Oriented, any
faculty's lecture — your faculty is handing you gold. Important concepts framed
the exact right way. The traps the examiner sets. How to build your working
notes for that chapter. Most students let this pass. You collect it.

For every chapter, maintain one page — in your notebook or a separate section:

```
GOLDEN NUGGETS — [Subject] — [Chapter]

Important Concepts:    (with ref — "see P01-002, p.14")
Exam Tips:             (how to build working notes for this chapter)
Examiner Tricks:       (what the paper-setter does to trap students here)
```

These pages fold naturally into your Layer 2 when you build it.

If your Layer 2 already captures all of this — you already have Booti 1.
You're ahead. If not — build it now, chapter by chapter, while watching.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> "The most valuable things a teacher says are the throwaway lines — 'students
> always mess up this step,' 'this is where the examiner puts the trap,' 'write
> your working note like this, not like that.' Those lines are gold. I wrote them
> down the moment they were said. Half my edge in the exam came from those lines."

---

### Strategy 5 — Sanjeevani Booti 2: The Error Register [ALL]

After watching the revision video, when you sit to practice — every mistake
goes here. Not in your head. Not "I'll remember this." Here. Written.
Chapter by chapter. Subject by subject.

You can keep it as a **separate register**, or leave **3–4 pages at the end
of each chapter** in your existing notebook — your choice.

**Error entry format:**
```
Question No · Source · What I did · Why I did it · What was true · Why it was true
```

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> When you're in revision mode, use these specific fields — they make each entry
> instantly findable when the error register becomes your primary revision tool:
> ```
> Where is this question?    (book + page / Drive file + page)
> Where is the solution?     (exact location)
> What was asked?            (one line)
> Where did I mess up?       (the exact step or concept)
> Why did I mess up?         (what I thought vs. what it actually is)
> ```
> The "why did I mess up" line is the most important. Writing it forces you to
> understand the gap — not just note that one exists.

**While practising, flag your questions:** when you solve a question, tape a
small A4 flag-sheet into your answer — marking whether it was straightforward
or had a real concept/trick worth rechecking.

**How you use this register:** before you revise any chapter, read its error
entries first. You are priming yourself for the exact traps that have already
caught you. This single habit separates students who keep repeating mistakes
from students who stop.

---

### Strategy 6 — Sanjeevani Booti 3: Paste It Where You Live [ALL]

The concepts you keep forgetting shouldn't live only inside a notebook you have
to open. Put them where your eyes already go.

Printout or plain A4 paper. Write or print the concept — a flowchart, a table,
a mnemonic, whatever form makes it stick. Then paste:
- Above the washbasin where you brush — you'll read it twice a day without trying
- In the hallway between your study room and the breakfast table
- Near the dinner table
- Near your study table (last resort — eyes are already tired here)

When a sheet's content is genuinely locked — you know it without looking —
take it down. Put up the next weak one.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> "I pasted the GST place-of-supply rules in the hallway I walked through to eat.
> The front window was covered with mnemonics and rules. On both sides of the
> mirror where I brushed, I kept the errors I made most and the concepts that
> confused me most — and once something was genuinely memorised, I'd pull it down
> and put up the next one."

**This is exactly where AI changes what's possible in this phase.**

Use AI (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, KIMI, NotebookLM — for Hindi:
Indus / Sarvam AI) for:
- Building mnemonics using your favourite character, movie, or domain —
  personal hooks stick far better than generic ones
- Generating printable one-page summary sheets for your paste-it walls
- Making revision checklists per chapter
- Quizzing you one question at a time: *"I'm revising [topic]. Ask me the
  5 most commonly tested points, one at a time. Wait for my answer."*
- Explaining why your wrong MCQ option was wrong, not just why the right one
  is right

> **WARNING:**
> AI is a revision and practice tool — for mnemonics, quizzing, and making
> retention faster. What to study and the first real clarity on a concept must
> come from your faculty. Don't outsource your boundary or your foundational
> understanding to AI. Use the teacher to *understand*; use AI to *drill,
> test, and retain* what you've understood.
> Never blindly trust AI on exact figures or the latest law — verify against
> your Study Material.

### REVISION STRATEGIES

---

### Strategy 7 — Build Your Layer 2 [ALL]

One clean notebook per subject. Chapter by chapter, write your compressed notes.
It holds:
- Summarised concept notes (where Layer 1 is already clear, just point to it —
  "see P01-002, p.14" — don't rewrite)
- Your error register entries
- Exam tricks and mnemonics
- Important-question summaries

Table of contents at the front. Every page must earn its place.

**Write these notes in the language your brain thinks in.** Hindi or Hinglish
is not just allowed, it's better. These notes are for you to revise from, not
to submit. In the exam, your answers stay in proper English — but your notes
are yours.

At the start of each chapter, tape in your **revision flow** for that chapter —
what to revise, which questions to hit, from where.

**Compression cuts time, not content.** Layer 2 is shorter so you can read it
faster and understand more per minute. It does NOT mean dropping chapters.
Every chapter in your boundary stays. You're summarising, not deleting.

---

### Strategy 8 — Revise, Then Test — That's How You Cover RTP/MTP/PYQ [ALL]

Revision isn't just re-reading. Every time you sit to revise a chapter, have
a test plan ready before you start.

> **HOW TO DO THIS:**
> Before opening your notes for a chapter, decide the test: "after this revision
> I'll solve these 3 questions / attempt these 10 MCQs / do a blind sheet / let
> AI quiz me." Do the test before moving on. This is exactly how your RTP, MTP,
> and PYQ coverage happens — each chapter's revision is "done" only when its
> matching past-paper questions have been attempted as the test. Revision and
> past-paper coverage are the same activity, not two separate ones.

---

### Strategy 9 — The Error Register Becomes Your Edge [ALL]

By now your error register is filling up. Use it. Before each chapter's revision,
read its error entries first — you're priming yourself for the exact traps that
have already caught you. This single habit separates students who keep repeating
mistakes from students who stop.

---

### Strategy 10 — Rate Your Chapters [RANK]

Once a week, give every chapter a quick three-part rating:

```
1. Exam Relevant — "kitni baar exam mein aata hai?"   →  A / B / C
   (from your weightage analysis — reuse it)
2. Recall        — "topics kitne achhe se yaad hai?"  →  ___ / 10
3. Simulation    — "question solve ho raha hai?"      →  ___ / 10
```

The danger chapter is an **A** sitting at low Recall or low Simulation. That
gap is exactly where your next hours go. A C-chapter at 4/10 can wait; an
A-chapter at 4/10 is a mark-leak waiting to happen. Re-rate every week.

---

### Strategy 11 — Practise Like the Exam, Not Like a Stopwatch [ALL]

Speed-solving in your head is not enough. Every day you practise a practical
subject, some questions must be done in full exam-pattern mode — proper ruling,
proper working notes, proper format — exactly as you'd write in the hall.

A selection of questions in exam mode, speed-revision for the rest. Quality
of a few, awareness of the many.

> **RANK ONLY:**
> Sit for at least one full examination-mode simulation in this phase — a
> complete MTP, 3 hours, 15-minute reading time, written exactly like the real
> exam. Analyse the result with four questions: Where did I lose marks? Concept,
> calculation, presentation, or time? What do I fix? Did I leave blanks?

---

### Strategy 12 — Solve It Yourself, Not via Solution Videos [ALL]

Solution videos show someone else solving. They build their skill, not yours.
Watch one and you feel you understand; try the question next day and the
feeling's gone. Train your own brain to sit with confusion and crack it.
The exam hall has no solution video.

---

### Strategy 13 — Match Subject to Your Energy [ALL]

Give your most productive window — whenever that is for you, morning or night —
to **theory**. Theory needs a fresh, alert brain to actually go in and stay.
When you're feeling low or sleepy, switch to **practical sums** — the active
calculation keeps you awake far better than reading theory would.
Find your own peak and guard it for whatever is hardest for you to retain.

---

### Strategy 14 — MCQs, Properly [ALL]

For every MCQ — right or wrong — know why the right answer is right and why
each wrong option is wrong. That's where real clarity is built. Finish the ICAI
MCQ booklet and one faculty MCQ bank, twice each.

> **HOW TO DO THIS:**
> After any MCQ: *"I chose [option]; correct is [option]. Explain why each of
> the other three is wrong — not just why the right one is right."* Ask this
> to your AI tool. The explanation of the wrong options is where the exam tests
> you.

---

### Strategy 15 — Paste It Where You Live (Non-Negotiable in This Phase) [ALL]

[REF: Sanjeevani Booti 3 — Strategy 6 above]

Run Booti 3 actively throughout this phase. Every concept you find yourself
forgetting repeatedly goes on a sheet. Every sheet that's locked comes down.
By the end of First 45 Days, your walls should be thinning — not growing.
A wall that keeps growing means you're not actually retaining. Fix the
retention method, don't just keep adding sheets.

---

### Strategy 16 — A Fixed Revision Sequence [ALL]

Pick one chapter order per subject and stick to it. Constantly reshuffling
wastes energy on re-orientation and feels like variety while delivering nothing.

---

### Strategy 17 — Recovery and Restart [ALL]

Bad days, illness, family disruptions — universal. The skill isn't avoiding
them. It's restarting fast. One missed day is a missed day, not a failed attempt.

> **HOW TO DO THIS:**
> Don't try to "make up" a lost day by cramming double the next day — that
> just breaks two days instead of one. Adjust the plan forward, trim scope if
> you must, keep the system running. A missed day is a calendar problem, not
> a verdict on you.

### THE NORTH STAR REVISITED

You have been asking this question every day:

> **What will I study in the 21 hours before my exam?**

In this phase, that question gets a specific, named answer: your **Layer 3 book**.

By the time you walk into the exam centre, you must be able to flip through your
Layer 3 book and say — for every subject, every chapter — exactly what you will
revise in those final hours. If your Layer 3 doesn't do that, it isn't ready.

---

### Strategy 1 — Build Layer 3 [ALL]

Layer 3 is the glue — the high-level revision flow that lets you move through a
whole subject fast. One thin bound notebook per subject (or one bound book with
clear subject sections). A bound book beats loose sheets — pages can't fall out,
can't get out of order, and it's easy to carry to the exam centre.

- **Practical subjects:** chapter-wise revision flow — what to revise, which
  questions to hit, from where. ("SM Illustration 7", "P01-Layer2, p.23")
- **Theory subjects:** question hints + mnemonics, in revision order.
- **Law-type subjects:** section numbers, penalty clauses, time limits, monetary
  limits, due dates — in the order you'll revise them.

The test of a good Layer 3: you can read a full subject in about 10 minutes
(practical) or 15–20 (theory/law). Longer than that — it's Layer 2 in disguise.
Compress more.

This bound book is what you carry into the exam hall.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> If you haven't built a custom revision sequence, use this default:
> ```
> Step 1 — Category A chapters first  (highest marks across past papers)
> Step 2 — Category B chapters next
> Step 3 — Category C chapters last
> ```
> Don't overthink the sequence — this default beats a blank page every time.
> Adjust it after your first mock when you know where the gaps are.

---

### Strategy 2 — No New Material [ALL]

If it wasn't in your boundary at the Day-45 Checkpoint, it won't be prepared
well enough to help — and chasing it costs you revision time on what you *do*
know. Secure what you have. The boundary is closed.

---

### Strategy 3 — Depth Over Breadth [ALL]

A-category: 100%. These carry the bulk of the marks — you can't be shaky here.
B-category: solid, but not at the cost of A.
C-category: only after A and B are locked.

A student who owns A-category cold will out-score a student who did everything
at 60% depth — and won't understand why, after "preparing everything."

> **RANK ONLY:**
> Rankers don't get to choose. 100% across A, B, and C. No shortcuts.
> If it's in the syllabus, it's prepared.

---

### Strategy 4 — Daily Format Drills [ALL]

For practical subjects: 20 minutes each morning, draw the key formats from
memory — balance sheet, cash flow, consolidation, P&L, whatever applies.
Under exam pressure a half-remembered format costs you minutes and confidence.
An automatic one costs nothing.

> **HOW TO DO THIS:**
> Blank sheet each morning, draw the format from memory, check against Layer 2,
> log anything missed into your error register. Repeat tomorrow.

---

### Strategy 5 — Mock Test + Analysis Cycle [ALL]

Full 3-hour mock every 3–4 days. After each one, an hour of honest analysis —
four questions only:

```
1. Where did I lose marks?
2. Concept, calculation, presentation, or time?
3. What exactly do I fix before the next mock?
4. Did I attempt everything, or did I leave blanks?
```

Roll the findings into your error register.

> **RANK ONLY:**
> Track time per question across mocks. Find the question types eating
> disproportionate time and adjust your hall strategy.

---

### Strategy 6 — Error Register + Important Questions, Front and Centre [ALL]

In this phase the error register becomes your primary revision tool — start
every revision session with it. Alongside it, keep your important-questions
list open: the questions you flagged through the First 45 Days to definitely
recheck now. Together they are the most targeted revision material you own.

---

### Strategy 7 — The Sunday Sweep [ALL]

Every Sunday, an hour flipping fast through all your Layer 2 notes. Not deep
study — maintenance. By exam week, nothing in Layer 2 should feel new.

---

### Strategy 8 — Print What You'll Revise Again and Again [ALL]

Physical beats screen in this phase. Print your summary charts, format templates,
key provision tables, mnemonic sheets.

**Skip a meal if you must — don't skip printing an important summary chart.**

---

### Strategy 9 — Hold Steady [ALL]

This is the highest-anxiety stretch. Comparison spikes; everyone around you
looks stressed. Go back to Bucket 0 — the Blinders and the emotion-parking
habits matter more here than anywhere else.

[REF: Bucket 0]

---

### Strategy 10 — Revision Marathons: Reinforce, Don't Catch Up [ALL]

A revision marathon — blitzing a whole subject's Layer 2 in one long sitting —
is a powerful *reinforcement* tool. Use it to link chapters, build speed, and
prove to yourself that you can hold a subject end to end.

What it is **not** is a catch-up tool. Never use a marathon to "cover" something
you never actually studied. That's panic with a timetable: you skim, you feel
busy, you retain almost nothing. If something is still un-studied this close in,
that's a boundary call — drop it, or give it a proper focused first-study.

# EXAM: THE 15 DAYS
## Pure Execution

> **END GOAL:**
> Nothing new is learned here. It's all about delivering what you've prepared
> and protecting your body and mind across the exam stretch. On track when,
> every paper:
> - [ ] Kit packed the night before; reached the centre early
> - [ ] Used the full 15-minute reading time to fix your sequence
> - [ ] MCQs done first; opened with your most confident question
> - [ ] Presentation + submission mechanics clean; nothing left blank
> - [ ] Slept 7–8 hours; didn't dissect the finished paper with anyone
> - [ ] Light Layer-3 + error-register pass before the next paper

**Prerequisite:** Layer 3 exists. Several revisions done. Some mock experience.

> **STARTING LATE? BARE MINIMUM:**
> No Layer 3? Carry your Layer 2, or your best notes. No mocks done? Treat your
> first paper as the mock — watch what goes wrong, adjust for the papers that
> follow. You still have Bucket 0 running every day.

### SET THIS UP BEFORE THE EXAMS BEGIN

---

### Strategy 1 — Your Exam Kit and Logistics [ALL]

An entire attempt can be lost to a dead calculator or a misread bubble — not
to weak preparation. Sort this out *before* the exams start, then repeat the
night-before checklist for each paper.

**One-time, before the exams:**
- **Scout the centre** — visit once, time the commute, find your block. No
  surprises on Day 1.
- **Two identical calculators** — same brand, same model. Muscle memory carries
  over; a battery failure can't stop you. Identical matters — a different key
  layout mid-exam costs time and rhythm.
- **3–4 photocopies of your hall ticket** — keep spares; leave one with a parent.
- Buy stationery: **3–4 black ball pens of the same brand**, two pencils, eraser,
  small and big scale, and a **stapler**. Compass box if your paper needs it.

**The night before each paper:**
- Pack the bag and full kit the night before — never the morning of. Zero
  morning decisions.
- For the **first paper, reach at least an hour early** to settle in.
- Wear **loose, comfortable clothing**.
- Eat light on exam day — idli/poha, two rotis — so you don't crash mid-paper.
  Carry **nimbu-paani / glucose / a couple of sweets** for the 3-hour window.

### Strategy 6 — Layer 3 at the Centre [ALL]

Reach early. Flip through your Layer 3 book — 20–30 minutes, the whole subject.

### INSIDE THE HALL

---

### Strategy 7 — Use the 15 Minutes Reading Time [ALL]

Before writing begins you get 15 minutes of reading time. This is not a break —
it is where the paper is half-won. Scan the whole paper and mark every question:
✓ (know it well, will attempt), ? (know parts, maybe), ✗ (skip). Then decide
two things: your **sequence**, and roughly how long each question gets.

> **RANK ONLY:**
> Pencil the time plan lightly on the question paper — "Q1: 25 min, Q2: 20 min"
> — so you're executing a plan, not improvising mid-paper.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> "I always used the reading time to pencil-scan the whole paper and see exactly
> what's asked. The first job is to fix the sequence. Then I'd open with the
> question I was most confident in — a strong first answer sets the impression
> on the examiner."

---

### Strategy 8 — Perseverance, at the Highest Level [ALL]

There will be questions you read and your mind goes blank — even things you
revised yesterday. This is normal under pressure. Do not panic. Breathe, move
to a question you can do, and come back. The exam tests nerve as much as
knowledge. Hold steady through the full 3 hours.

---

### Strategy 9 — MCQs First [ALL]

Always start with the MCQ section. It warms up the brain, secures those marks
early, and builds confidence before the big subjective questions.

- For numerical MCQs: calculate first, then match — never eliminate by guesswork.
- Can't crack a numerical MCQ in ~90 seconds? Mark it, move on, return later.
- No negative marking — never leave an MCQ blank. A reasoned guess beats empty.

---

### Strategy 10 — Build Momentum [ALL]

After MCQs, start with the question you're most confident about. Strong early
answers steady your nerves. Don't open with the hardest one. Hit the hard
questions once you're in flow, ~45–60 minutes in.

---

### Strategy 11 — Presentation and Submission [ALL]

Readable handwriting — not pretty, readable. Working notes numbered and labelled
right below each answer. Every statement and account gets a heading. Cite the
standard/section when you use it ("As per AS 16…"). Underline final answers and
key terms — only those.

The small mechanics that quietly cost marks:
- **Tick attempted-question boxes** on the cover — every one you attempt.
  An un-ticked answer can go unmarked.
- On the MCQ/OMR sheet, **align every bubble carefully** — one row out of place
  and every answer after it is wrong.
- Before submitting, **count your supplements** so every extra sheet is attached.
- Keep the **pen cap off the back** while writing — less weight, less fatigue.
- Save the **last ~10 minutes to review** — check totals, underline final answers,
  confirm nothing is half-marked.

> **RANK ONLY:**
> Add disclosure notes even when not asked — each valid one can carry half a mark
> to a mark, and signals depth.

---

### Strategy 12 — Never Leave It Blank [ALL]

Stuck? Show what you can — the opening entry, the format headings, working
note 1. State your approach in a line: "Proceeding with Purchase Method as per
AS 14." Partial marks are real. An empty page is a zero. A half-attempt is
3–5 marks.

---

### Strategy 13 — Time Discipline [ALL]

No single question gets more than 1.5× its allotted time before you move on.
Forty minutes on a 12-mark question while three others sit blank is an expensive
mistake.

---

### Strategy 14 — When the Balance Sheet Won't Tally [ALL]

Don't erase everything. Write: "Note: difference of ₹___, likely from [area];
approach and working notes are correct." Examiners reward honesty and award step
marks for correct working notes and schedules even when the final figure is off.

---

### BETWEEN PAPERS

---

### Strategy 15 — Don't Discuss the Paper [ALL]

Walk out, don't dissect it with anyone. Finding out someone wrote something
differently only breeds anxiety you can't act on. That energy belongs to the
next paper.

---

### Strategy 16 — Keep Sleeping 7–8 Hours [ALL]

Exam week is the time to sleep most *consistently*, not least. Every paper
deserves a rested brain.

---

### Strategy 17 — Light Prep for the Next Paper [ALL]

After resting, do light evening revision for the next paper — Layer 3 + error
register for that subject. You're reminding, not relearning.

### Strategy 1 — The Second Space in Your Phone [ALL]

Your phone is your study tool now — your classes, notes, PDFs, and AI all live
on it. Split it.

Create a **Second Space / New User** on your phone. A clean study-only profile
inside your phone, for these preparation months.

In this space, install only what serves the exam:
- Your faculty's app
- Companies Act / GST / Income Tax apps
- AI tools
- A water reminder, a posture reminder
- A news app

Instagram, WhatsApp chats — they stay in your main space. This space is
study only.

> **HOW TO DO THIS:**
> Android: Settings → Users / Second Space → create new user → install only the
> apps above.
>
> iPhone / iOS: iOS doesn't support a second user. Hide and lock distracting apps,
> turn off their notifications, mute the groups, and filter contacts so nothing
> pulls you mid-study.

### Strategy 2 — AI Tools, Installed and Ready [ALL]

Install in your Second Space: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, KIMI, and
an India-native option like Indus / Sarvam AI (good for Hindi). The full playbook
on how to use AI for revision is in the First 45 Days section and in Strategy 6
(Sanjeevani Booti 3). [REF: Sanjeevani Booti 3]

---

### Strategy 3 — Sleep [ALL]

7–8 hours. Every night. Your brain reinforces everything you studied while you
sleep. Cut sleep and you cut the reinforcement.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> "I have personally tried polyphasic sleep and multiple other sleep strategies.
> Nothing worked and nothing was required. The only thing that worked: a fixed
> sleep timing, without fail, and 6 hours fixed sleep at night plus a 20–30
> minute nap during the day. Timings may differ for everyone — but minimum 6
> hours and maximum 8 hours is non-negotiable."

---

### Strategy 4 — Move Your Body [ALL]

15–20 minutes of movement daily — walk, stretch, yoga, badminton, anything.
Every 30 minutes of sitting: roll your neck, stretch your back.

**Eyes — the 20-20-20 rule:** every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away
for 20 seconds.

> **DIAGRAM: Posture and Eye Exercise**
> One page of line-drawing diagrams: neck rolls, shoulder shrugs, back stretch,
> wrist rotations, palming, eye rolling.

---

### Strategy 5 — Hydration and Light Meals [ALL]

Water through the day. Dehydration kills focus before you even notice it.

Watch the plate before you sit to study. A heavy meal pulls blood to your stomach
and your eyes start closing inside twenty minutes. Eat lighter before study
blocks. Food that keeps you sharp: sprouts, roasted chana, curd, banana, peanuts,
soaked almonds, paneer, makhana, light poha or idli.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> "I never drank coffee or tea until my first year of articleship. My drinks during
> preparation were water, lemon-salt water, jeera-lemon-salt water, and coconut
> water. Simple. Cheap. Worked."

---

### Strategy 6 — Wear Your Blinders [ALL]

There is exactly one thing in front of you: your Dream Marksheet. No topper
celebrations, no comparing whose syllabus is further along, no doing the maths
on someone else's progress.

> **HOW TO DO THIS:**
> - Mute every WhatsApp group where batchmates compare progress. Mute, don't leave.
> - When family asks how prep is going, one answer: "On track." Don't elaborate.
> - When someone posts a celebration, reply "Congratulations." Then move on.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> "In my Foundation internal exams, there was a girl who beat me about three times.
> I was the first to congratulate her every single time. Only I know how that felt.
> But I never let it turn into anything ugly inside me. She became one of my best
> friends. Your real competition is you."

---

### Strategy 7 — Park Your Emotions Before You Study [ALL]

Whatever is going on outside — family, a fight, a hard day — it stays outside
the study room. Not because it doesn't matter. Because you can't fix it in the
next two hours, and you can definitely lose those two hours to it.

> **HOW TO DO THIS:**
> Before you sit, take 2 minutes. Write down what's bothering you on a scrap
> of paper — complete detail, whatever comes to mind. Fold it. It's parked.
> Store it in paper, not in your mind. Open your notebook. Come back later.

---

### Strategy 8 — The 10-Minute Flashback (Before Sleep) [ALL]

Don't end the night doom-scrolling. Lie down, eyes closed, and replay your
whole day. What happened, what you studied, what you understood. Not judging
it good or bad. Just recalling, in detail. You're training your brain's recall
muscle — the exact muscle the exam tests.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> "If you overthink like I do, here's my hack. Pick the one concept or problem you
> didn't understand that day and just turn it over in your head — no Google, no
> notes, only your brain — until you fall asleep. I solved a lot of maths problems
> this way during Foundation."

---

### Strategy 9 — Talk to Yourself (Daily Journal, GenZ Version) [ALL]

Forget writing a diary. Make a WhatsApp group with only yourself. At the end
of the day, send a voice note — say whatever you're feeling, what went well,
what didn't. Just talk.

---

### Strategy 10 — Daily Targets, Not a Daily Lecture [ALL]

Morning: write today's actual targets. Not "study accounts." Specific: "finish
Consolidation illustrations 4–9, note errors, mark important questions."

Night: in your self-WhatsApp note, check what got done, what didn't, why.
Carry the misses forward honestly.

**The point is the target, not the hours.** Never measure your day by "I sat
for 12 hours." Measure it by "did today's targets get done."

> **RANK ONLY:**
> Keep a weekly tally — hours, chapters, questions. Review every Sunday.
> Adjust next week on data, not mood.

---

### Strategy 11 — Earn Your Breaks [ALL]

Parties, functions, outings — yes, but when your week's targets are on track.
This isn't punishment. It's deciding in advance that the fun follows the work.

---

### Strategy 12 — Keep a Hobby [ALL]

Continue at least one hobby through these preparation months.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> "Keep a hobby. Otherwise the articleship interview gets very boring — mine was
> quite boring. Lolzz" (Said with a smile. But he's not fully joking.)

---

### Strategy 13 — Make It a Game [ALL]

Learning without a target is just punishment. Learning without fun is incomplete.

Think of your preparation as a game with levels: each phase you complete is a
level cleared. The exam is the Final Boss. Every chapter you nail, every error
register entry you write, every Sanjeevani Booti you complete — that's XP earned.

The goal isn't to *survive* the preparation. It's to *play* it well.

> **PRANAV'S TIP:**
> "I always kept a mental scoreboard. Not comparing with anyone else — just
> against my own yesterday. Did I do more today? Did I understand something
> I didn't understand last week? That small internal game kept the energy alive
> across months. Find your own version of 'Princess ko bachana hai' — you just
> have to find your own princess."

---

Bucket 0 isn't a phase you finish — it's a list you run every single day.
Copy this onto a sheet, stick it on your wall, and tick it each night:

Missed a box? No drama — tick it tomorrow. The streak is the point, not
perfection.
