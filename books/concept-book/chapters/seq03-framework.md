## THE STORY — "Byju's Ka Passport"

### Scene 1 — The Call That Changed Everything

It was a Tuesday morning. Arjun had been awake since 5 AM.

Not because of anxiety — this time, curiosity. Class 1 had ended with a picture in his head that he couldn't shake: a chain. Banks, foreign investors, employees, students, government — all connected. One wrong entry in one company's accounts had pulled at every link. He had gone home and looked up BlackRock. Then Prosus. Then Sequoia. These were not small players.

*These people had put billions of dollars into Byju's. They had read the accounts. They had trusted the numbers. And still — it had collapsed.*

That question would not leave him: **how do you read accounts made in a country whose rules you don't know?**

When Pranav Bhaiya arrived that morning, Arjun asked before he had even sat down.

"Bhaiya — yeh jo US investors the, BlackRock aur Sequoia — unhe toh India ke accounting standards pata nahi honge? Toh unhe accounts samjha kaise?"

Pranav set down his coffee and looked at him with a specific kind of satisfaction — the kind a teacher gets when a student asks the right question.

"Arjun — yeh sabse important question hai. Aur iska jawab do hisso mein hai. Pehle: India ek ऐसा system bana raha tha jisme Indian accounts globally readable ho sakein — use Ind AS kehte hain, hum baad mein dekhenge. But doosra aur zyada fundamental question hai: **Koi bhi accounting standard kahan se shuru hota hai? Uske peeche kya sochke banaya?**"

"Matlab?"

"Matlab yeh. Har desh ke apne laws hain. India mein Companies Act. USA mein SEC rules. Germany mein HGB. Par har law ke peeche — ek constitution hota hai. Ek set of fundamental ideas jiske upar baki sab kuch build hota hai. Accounting mein bhi wahi hai. Use kehte hain — **the Framework for Preparation and Presentation of Financial Statements.**"

Arjun looked up. "Ek constitution? Accounts ke liye?"

"Bilkul. ICAI ne yeh Framework July 2000 mein issue kiya. Yeh document — koi specific rule nahi batata. Koi specific standard nahi. Yeh batata hai: **why** financial statements are prepared. **Who** uses them. **What** the fundamental building blocks are. **How** you measure things. Jab bhi ASB — Accounting Standards Board — ek naya standard banata hai, woh is Framework ko dekh ke banata hai. Like a Constitution — specific laws change, but the principles under them stay."

### Scene 2 — The Byju's Course Fee Problem

Pranav picked up a marker and wrote on the whiteboard:

**BYJU'S — ₹90,000 received from student. 3-year course. Year 1.**

"Arjun — ek simple question. Byju's ne ₹90,000 liya. Class 1 mein humne kaha tha ki yeh galat tha poora Year 1 mein book karna. Par mujhe bata — **fundamentally** — yeh ₹90,000 kya hai? Asset hai? Income hai? Liability hai?"

Arjun thought for a moment. "Income hoga na? Paisa aa gaya."

"Paisa aa gaya — agreed. Par course deliver hua? Teen saal ki obligation hai Byju's ki. Yeh ₹90,000 toh pehle ek **liability** hai — ek promise kiya hai ki hum teen saal padhayenge. Jab tak woh promise puri nahi hoti, income book nahi hogi. Jab course deliver hoga — tab income."

Arjun nodded slowly. "Toh pehle liability. Phir income."

"Exactly. Par tum yeh argue kar paaye kyunki tumhare paas ek framework tha — **ek definition of income, ek definition of liability.** Agar yeh definitions nahi hoti, toh Byju's ka management boleta: *'Paisa toh aa gaya, income book kar lo.'* Aur koi rok nahi sakta tha."

"Framework ne define kiya ki **income kya hoti hai.** Specific AS 9 — Revenue Recognition — aaya framework ke baad, uski same definitions ko law mein convert karke. Framework Constitution hai, AS 9 us Constitution ke under ka specific law."

### [FW-1] Introduction — What the Framework Is

The Framework for Preparation and Presentation of Financial Statements is NOT an Accounting Standard. It does not prescribe specific accounting treatments. It sets out the **underlying concepts** on which all Accounting Standards are built.

Issued by: **Accounting Standards Board (ASB) of ICAI — July 2000.**

The Framework covers:
1. Components of financial statements
2. Objectives of financial statements
3. Assumptions underlying financial statements
4. Qualitative characteristics of financial statements
5. Elements of financial statements (Asset, Liability, Equity, Income, Expense)
6. Criteria for recognition of elements
7. Principles for measurement
8. Concepts of Capital and Capital Maintenance

**Key rule:** In case of conflict between an Accounting Standard and the Framework — **the Accounting Standard prevails.** The Framework is foundational, but specific standards are authoritative.

---

### [FW-2] Purpose of the Framework

The Framework assists six groups:

| Who | How the Framework Helps |
|---|---|
| **Enterprises** | Prepare financial statements compliant with AS; handle topics not covered by any AS yet |
| **ASB** | Develop and review Accounting Standards |
| **ASB again** | Reduce the number of alternative treatments permitted, promoting harmonisation |
| **Auditors** | Form an opinion on whether financial statements conform to AS |
| **Users** | Interpret financial statements |
| **Interested parties** | Understand ASB's approach to standard-setting |

> **Story Reference —** In the opening story, Pranav explains that Framework is the Constitution of Accounting — every standard is built on it, every interpretation of an unusual transaction goes back to it. When a company faces a transaction for which no specific AS exists yet, the Framework fills the gap.

---

### [FW-3] Status and Scope

The Framework applies to **general-purpose financial statements** — those prepared annually for external users, by all commercial, industrial and business enterprises (public or private sector).

**What is OUT of scope:** Special-purpose reports — for example, computations prepared for income tax purposes. However, the Framework *may* be applied to these reports to the extent not inconsistent with their requirements.

**The hierarchy:** Accounting Standard > Framework. The Framework is foundational, but never overrides a specific standard.

---

### [FW-4] Components of Financial Statements

A complete set of financial statements consists of:

| Component | Primary Information |
|---|---|
| **Balance Sheet** | Value of economic resources controlled by the enterprise; liquidity and solvency position |
| **Statement of Profit and Loss** | Results of operations; profitability for the period |
| **Cash Flow Statement** | How cash was generated and used; investing, financing and operating activity analysis |
| **Notes and Other Statements** | Supplementary information — accounting policies, segment reporting, related party disclosures, EPS, etc. |

Key principle: **All components are interrelated.** They reflect different aspects of the same transactions. No single statement in isolation can serve all needs.

---

### [FW-5] Objectives and the Seven Users of Financial Statements

**Objective:** Provide information about financial position, performance and cash flows of an enterprise — useful to a wide range of users in making economic decisions.

The Framework identifies **7 broad groups of users:**

| User | What They Are Looking For |
|---|---|
| **Investors** | Risk and return on investment; ability of enterprise to pay dividends |
| **Employees** | Stability and profitability; remuneration, retirement benefits, employment continuity |
| **Lenders** | Whether loans and interest will be repaid when due |
| **Suppliers and Trade Creditors** | Whether amounts owing will be paid when due |
| **Customers** | Continuance of enterprise, especially for long-term product/service dependency |
| **Governments and agencies** | Allocation of resources; regulation; taxation policy; national income statistics |
| **Public** | Contribution to local economy; employment; patronage of local suppliers |

> **Story Reference —** In the opening story, Pranav draws seven chairs. Each chair is a user of Byju's financial statements. Each user was failed by the same set of misstated accounts. The Framework identifies these users precisely because every accounting decision must ultimately serve them.

**What financial statements cannot do:** They cannot provide all information a user needs. Non-financial data (quality of management, brand value, employee morale) is outside the scope of financial statements.

---

### [FW-6] Fundamental Accounting Assumptions

Three assumptions underlie all financial statements. If they are followed, **no separate disclosure is required.** If any is NOT followed, **that fact must be specifically disclosed.**

#### (a) Going Concern
Financial statements are prepared assuming the enterprise will **continue in operation in the foreseeable future** — no intention or need to materially curtail the scale of operations.

Implication: Assets are valued at cost less depreciation (not at liquidation value). Deferred revenue expenditure is spread over multiple years. Liabilities are classified as current or non-current based on normal business continuity.

If NOT followed: Assets are shown at net realisable value. All deferred expenditure is written off immediately. Loan penalties and break costs are recognised. Long-term liabilities become current. **The financial picture changes dramatically.**

> **Exam Note:** Illustration 1 of the ICAI chapter is a high-priority numerical question — it shows the difference between a Going Concern P&L/Balance Sheet and a Not-Going-Concern P&L/Balance Sheet for the same enterprise. Every line changes. TYK Q10 is a full scenario version of this. Master this.

#### (b) Accrual
Revenues and costs are **recognised when earned or incurred** — not when cash is received or paid. They are recorded in the financial statements of the periods to which they relate.

Section 128(1) of Companies Act 2013: **Mandatory** for companies to maintain accounts on accrual basis only.

Implication: Outstanding expenses are liabilities even if unpaid. Prepaid expenses are assets even if already paid. Income earned but not received is a receivable.

> **Story Reference —** Byju's received ₹90,000 upfront for a 3-year course. Accrual basis says: recognise income only as the service is delivered. Booking the full ₹90,000 in Year 1 was an accrual violation — the income was not yet earned.

> **Kaam Ki Baat — Accrual at Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas**
>
> At PPAC (Petroleum Planning and Analysis Cell) — which operates under the Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas — the Government of India makes huge subsidy payments to oil companies for selling petrol, diesel, and LPG below cost. But the actual subsidy disbursement from the Union Budget happens months after the financial year ends.
>
> When was the subsidy recognised in the oil company's books?
>
> On accrual basis — in the year the subsidy was **earned** (i.e., the year the fuel was sold below cost). Not in the year the cash was received. This meant that IOCL, BPCL, and HPCL had to show "Subsidy Receivable from Government of India" as an asset on their balance sheets — sometimes in thousands of crores — waiting for the actual cash to arrive.
>
> The financial statements told the truth: *this money is owed to us, for work we have already done.* The cash had not come. The income had.
>
> This is accrual. The earnings event drives the recognition — not the payment event.

#### (c) Consistency
Accounting policies are assumed to be **consistent from one period to another.** Consistency enables comparability of financial statements across time.

An accounting policy **can** be changed only if required:
1. By a statute, or
2. By an Accounting Standard, or
3. For more appropriate presentation of financial statements

If changed, the financial effect of the change must be disclosed.

---

### [FW-7] Qualitative Characteristics of Financial Statements

Four attributes that improve the usefulness of financial information:

#### 1. Understandability
Information must be presented in a manner readily understandable by users with **reasonable knowledge** of business, economics and accounting. This does not mean complex information can be omitted — it means complex information must be explained clearly.

#### 2. Relevance
Information that is likely to **influence the economic decisions** of users is relevant. Irrelevant information creates confusion — it can be more harmful than non-disclosure.

**Materiality** is the threshold for relevance. An item is material if its misstatement (omission or erroneous statement) could influence economic decisions of a user. Materiality is judged by the **size and nature** of the item in the specific circumstances.

**Constraints on Relevance:**
- **Timeliness:** Delayed information loses relevance. But rushing to report before all facts are known impairs reliability. Balance is required.
- **Benefit vs Cost:** The benefits of information must exceed the cost of providing it. This is a pervasive constraint — not a qualitative characteristic itself.

#### 3. Reliability
Information must be **free from material error and bias**. Reliable information requires:
- **(a) Faithful representation** — transactions are reported as they actually occurred
- **(b) Substance over form** — reported based on economic reality, not just legal form
- **(c) Neutrality** — free from bias; presentation does not skew conclusions toward a pre-determined outcome
- **(d) Prudence** — caution in uncertain outcomes; anticipate no profits, provide for all losses
- **(e) Completeness** — material information is not omitted

> **Story Reference —** Byju's management violated every one of these. Faithful representation failed (income booked before earned). Substance ignored (form said "cash received", substance said "obligation outstanding"). Neutrality failed (accounts designed to attract investors). Prudence reversed (profits anticipated before earned). Completeness failed (obligation to deliver courses — a liability — was not disclosed properly).

#### 4. Comparability
Financial statements should permit **both inter-firm comparison** (same industry, different companies) and **intra-firm comparison** (same company, different years).

This requires consistent application of accounting policies AND disclosure of the financial effect when policies are changed.

**Important distinction:** Comparability ≠ Uniformity. A company should not keep using an inferior accounting policy just to maintain comparability. If a better, more relevant policy exists — change it, disclose the effect, move on.

---

### [FW-8] True and Fair View

Financial statements are required to show a **true and fair view** of performance, financial position and cash flows.

The Framework does not define "true and fair view" directly. However: application of the four qualitative characteristics (Understandability, Relevance, Reliability, Comparability) and appropriate Accounting Standards normally results in financial statements that portray a true and fair view.

---

### [FW-9] Elements of Financial Statements

The Framework classifies items into **5 elements** based on economic characteristics:

| Element | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Asset** | A resource **controlled** by the enterprise as a result of **past events** from which **future economic benefits** are expected to flow to the enterprise |
| **Liability** | A **present obligation** of the enterprise arising from **past events**, the settlement of which is expected to result in an **outflow** of resources embodying economic benefits |
| **Equity** | **Residual interest** in the assets of an enterprise after deducting all its liabilities (A − L = E) |
| **Income** | Increase in economic benefits during the accounting period through **inflows / enhancement of assets** or **decreases in liabilities** that result in increase in equity — **other than** contributions from equity participants |
| **Expense** | Decrease in economic benefits during the accounting period through **outflows / depletions of assets** or **incurrence of liabilities** that result in decrease in equity — **other than** distributions to equity participants |

**Key extensions:**
- Gains (income not in ordinary course) and Losses (expenses not in ordinary course) are NOT separate elements — they are included within Income and Expenses respectively
- Income is **always** associated with either increase in an asset or decrease in a liability — no income can be recognised without the corresponding asset/liability recognition
- Expenses are **always** accompanied by either a decrease in an asset or an increase in a liability

---

#### [FW-9.1] Asset — Five Conditions

1. The resource **need not have physical substance** — patents, copyrights, trade receivables are assets
2. An asset is **controlled**, not necessarily owned — a lessee recognises a finance-leased asset even without ownership; lessor does NOT recognise it
3. **Control must be sufficient** — specific management talent or expertise of an employee cannot be an asset (insufficient control)
4. **Probable future economic benefits** beyond the current accounting period — if benefit exhausted in current period, it is an expense (e.g., machinery purchased by a machinery dealer for resale)
5. **Reliably measurable cost or value**

---

#### [FW-9.2] Liability — Three Key Points

1. A **present obligation** — not a future commitment. A management decision to buy an asset in the future does not create a present obligation. Obligation arises when asset is delivered OR enterprise enters an **irrevocable agreement** to acquire it.
2. **Provisions** can be liabilities (e.g., provision for warranty — a probable present obligation). But **provisions for doubtful debts, depreciation, impairment** are NOT liabilities — they represent diminution in value of assets.
3. Liability is recognised only when outflow can be **anticipated** and **reliably measured**.

> **Story Reference —** In the opening story: Byju's received ₹90,000 for a 3-year course. Before the course is delivered, this is a **liability** — a present obligation arising from a past event (receipt of cash), expected to result in outflow of resources (delivering education). It only converts to income as the obligation is fulfilled period by period.

---

#### [FW-9.3] The A − L = E Relationship

A useful framework for understanding all transactions:

```
Closing Equity = Opening Equity + Capital Introduced + Income − Expenses − Drawings
```

Or equivalently: Profit = (Closing Assets − Closing Liabilities) − (Opening Assets − Opening Liabilities) − Capital Introduced + Drawings

This shows that **profit depends on how assets and liabilities are valued.** Change the valuation basis → reported profit changes. This is the conceptual foundation of Capital Maintenance (see FW-11).

---

### [FW-10] Measurement of Elements

Measurement = determining the **money value** at which an element is recognised in the balance sheet or P&L.

The Framework recognises **four measurement bases:**

| Basis | Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical Cost** | Amount paid / fair value at acquisition | Amount of proceeds received in exchange for the obligation |
| **Current Cost** | Amount that would be paid to acquire the same/equivalent asset currently | Undiscounted amount required to settle the obligation currently |
| **Realisable (Settlement) Value** | Amount currently realisable on orderly sale of the asset | Undiscounted amount expected to be paid on settlement in normal course |
| **Present Value** | Present value of future net cash inflows expected from the asset | Present value of future net cash outflows required to settle the liability |

**Current practice:** Accounting Standards primarily use **Historical Cost**, with modifications:
- Inventory → Lower of Cost and NRV (realisable value element introduced)
- PPE → Cost less depreciation (though Fair Value revaluation permitted)
- Financial instruments → Fair value in some cases

**Which basis to use:** All four bases may be used in varying combinations, subject to requirements of specific Accounting Standards.

> **Kaam Ki Baat — Why Measurement Basis Matters (D2C Startup CFO Advisory)**
>
> In 2022, while advising a D2C beauty startup on their pre-Series A fundraise, I saw this problem at close range.
>
> The startup had built a proprietary formula for a skincare product — developed over two years, with ₹40 lakh spent on R&D, lab testing, and formulation. Under historical cost: the asset was ₹40 lakh in capitalised R&D (if the conditions for capitalisation were met under AS 26).
>
> The investor's valuation model used something very different: they estimated the future cash flows the formula would generate over its commercial life, discounted back to present value. That number: ₹3.2 crore.
>
> Same formula. Same product.
> - Historical cost: ₹40 lakh
> - Present value of future cash flows: ₹3.2 crore
>
> Neither number is wrong. They answer different questions. Historical cost says: *what did we spend to create this?* Present value says: *what is this worth today given what it will earn?*
>
> The investor made their decision using present value. The balance sheet showed historical cost. Both were legitimate — for different purposes. The Framework recognises all four bases because different decisions need different answers.

### [FW-11] Capital Maintenance

**Capital** = net assets of a business.

A business must maintain its capital — i.e., dividends should not exceed profit after appropriate provision for replacement of assets consumed. The Companies Act prohibits paying dividends without providing for depreciation precisely for this reason.

**The problem:** Under rising prices, historical cost-based depreciation may not be enough to replace assets. Three alternative bases for checking capital maintenance:

#### (a) Financial Capital Maintenance at Historical Cost
Opening and closing assets at respective historical costs. If Retained Profit ≥ 0, capital is maintained. Works correctly only if prices do not rise.

#### (b) Financial Capital Maintenance at Current Purchasing Power
Opening equity at historical cost is **restated to closing prices** using an **average price index.** If Retained Profit ≥ 0 under this basis, the business has enough to replace assets at average closing prices. Limitation: average price index does not reflect specific asset price changes.

#### (c) Physical Capital Maintenance at Current Costs
Opening and closing assets are restated using **specific price indices** applicable to each asset. A positive retained profit ensures funds for replacement of each asset at its own specific closing price. Most realistic — but most complex.

**Key formula across all three:**
```
Retained Profit = Closing Capital (at chosen basis) − Opening Capital (at chosen basis) − Capital Introduced
```
If Retained Profit is negative → capital is not being maintained → drawings/dividends have been too high.

> **Exam Note:** Illustration 2 in the ICAI chapter (and TYK Q8) tests all three capital maintenance bases together for the same trader. This is the most common exam scenario for this topic. Master the mechanics: restate opening equity using the given index, compare with closing equity, compute retained profit.

## ILLUSTRATION GUIDE
*Use ICAI Study Material for full working. These notes tell you what each illustration tests and where students go wrong.*

**Illustration 1 — Going Concern vs Not Going Concern (Trader)**
Tests: How the same Balance Sheet and transactions produce fundamentally different P&L and Balance Sheet under (i) Going Concern and (ii) Not Going Concern assumptions.
Key differences:
- Depreciation: Going concern = SLM over remaining life. Not GC = Difference between book value and NRV.
- Deferred expenditure: Going concern = amortise per year. Not GC = write off entire remaining balance.
- Doubtful debts: Going concern = provision for uncertain debts. Not GC = write off all doubtful and dependent receivables immediately.
- Loan penalty: Going concern = not applicable (loan continuing). Not GC = recognised immediately as expense.
- Trade payables: Going concern = no discount. Not GC = likely settlement discount = gain.
Common mistake: Students forget that under Not Going Concern, ALL deferred expenditure is written off immediately (not just the current year's amortisation), and loan prepayment penalty becomes a current expense.

**Example 1 — Accrual vs Cash Basis (Article A and B)**
Tests: Conceptual difference between accrual and cash basis using a simple two-period, two-article scenario.
Key teaching point: Under cash basis — Period 1 shows ₹58,000 profit, Period 2 shows ₹47,500 loss. Under accrual — Period 1 shows ₹10,500 profit in both periods. The accrual result is rational; the cash basis result is irrational. Use this to make the concept visceral.

**Example 2 — Liability Recognition (Custom Machine, Binding Contract)**
Tests: Para on Liability — when a present obligation exists under a binding agreement, even if delivery has not happened. A liability is recognised for the full purchase price even if the asset will be scrapped on delivery (and the full amount charged to P&L as an expense).
Common mistake: Students want to wait for delivery to recognise the liability. Wrong — the binding irrevocable agreement creates the present obligation now.

**Examples 3 and 4 — A − L = E Transaction Analysis**
Tests: Tracking the effect of each transaction (capital introduction, income, liability settlement, wages, rent, drawings) on Assets, Liabilities and Equity.
These examples form the basis for TYK Q9 — a scenario-based version of the same.

**Example 5 — Historical Cost Measurement**
Tests: Simple — machine purchased for ₹7,00,000 is recorded at ₹7,00,000 even if replacement cost is now ₹25,00,000. Historical cost = acquisition price.

**Example 6 — Current Cost Measurement**
Tests: Machine acquired in $ at old exchange rate. Current cost = current Indian market price of the same machine (₹5,00,000), not the historical ₹4,90,000.

**Example 7 — Present Value (Value in Use)**
Tests: Present value of future cash flows from an asset at a given discount rate. Asset should not be carried above its recoverable value (= higher of value in use and net selling price). If carrying amount > recoverable value → asset is overstated.

**Illustration 2 — Capital Maintenance (All Three Bases)**
Tests: Same trader, same data — compute Retained Profit under (i) Historical Cost, (ii) Current Purchasing Power (using average index), (iii) Physical Capital (using specific index).
Common mistake: Students apply the specific index to closing equity rather than to opening equity. The restatement is always of **opening** equity (or opening assets) to closing-period prices. Closing equity stays at its actual value.

## LAYER 3 — One-Liners for Framework

1. The Framework is the **Constitution of Accounting** — it sets underlying concepts on which all AS are built. It is NOT an Accounting Standard.
2. **Framework vs AS conflict: AS prevails always.** The Framework is foundational, not overriding.
3. Framework issued by: **ASB of ICAI, July 2000.**
4. Scope: General-purpose financial statements for external users. Special-purpose reports (e.g., tax computations) are outside scope.
5. A complete set of financial statements = **Balance Sheet + P&L + Cash Flow Statement + Notes.**
6. Seven users: **Investors, Employees, Lenders, Suppliers/Creditors, Customers, Government, Public.**
7. Three fundamental accounting assumptions: **Going Concern, Accrual, Consistency.** If followed → no disclosure needed. If NOT followed → must disclose.
8. **Going Concern:** Enterprise will continue in operation; no intention or need to curtail scale.
9. **Accrual:** Revenues and costs recognised when earned/incurred, not when cash received/paid. Mandatory for companies under Companies Act 2013.
10. **Consistency:** Same accounting policies across periods. Can change only if required by statute / AS / for more appropriate presentation.
11. Four qualitative characteristics: **Understandability, Relevance, Reliability, Comparability.**
12. **Materiality** is the threshold for Relevance — an item is material if its misstatement could influence economic decisions.
13. **Reliability** requires: faithful representation, substance over form, neutrality, prudence, completeness.
14. **Comparability ≠ Uniformity.** Do not retain inferior policies just for consistency.
15. Five elements: **Asset, Liability, Equity, Income, Expense.** Gains = part of Income. Losses = part of Expenses.
16. **Asset:** Resource controlled (not necessarily owned) + past event + probable future economic benefit.
17. **Liability:** Present obligation (not future commitment) + past event + expected outflow.
18. **Equity = Assets − Liabilities.** Equity changes through: contributions from/distributions to owners + income earned + expenses incurred.
19. **Income** always accompanied by asset increase or liability decrease. **Expense** always accompanied by asset decrease or liability increase.
20. Four measurement bases: **Historical Cost, Current Cost, Realisable Value, Present Value.** Historical cost is primarily used; others applied as required by specific AS.
21. **Capital Maintenance:** Three approaches — Historical Cost (prices don't change assumption), Current Purchasing Power (average price index), Physical Capital (specific price indices).
22. **Retained Profit = Closing Capital − Opening Capital − Capital Introduced.** If negative → capital not maintained → drawings were excessive.
