### UNIT 1: Meaning and Scope of Accounting

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Understand the meaning, evolution, objectives, and functions of accounting
- Distinguish bookkeeping from accounting; identify sub-fields, users, and relationships with other disciplines
- Recognize limitations of accounting and the role of a Chartered Accountant

**Unit Overview:** Accounting as a comprehensive information system — from its ancient roots to the modern profession.

---

**1.1 Introduction**
Accounting as an organised body of knowledge; why accounting exists; the need for systematic recording of business transactions.

**1.2 Meaning of Accounting**
- AICPA definition (1941): art of recording, classifying, summarising transactions in monetary terms; interpreting results
- AAA definition: process of identifying, measuring, communicating economic information
- APB Statement No. 4: service activity; provides quantitative financial information
- **1.2.1 Procedural Aspects:** 6 sequential steps — Identifying, Recording, Classifying, Summarising, Analysing & Interpreting, Communicating

**1.3 Evolution of Accounting**
- Egypt 4000 BC (earliest record keeping)
- Luca Pacioli 1494 (Summa de Arithmetica — double entry first published)
- 4 phases of evolution: ancient record keeping → mercantile accounting → industrial revolution → modern financial accounting

**1.4 Objectives of Accounting** (5 objectives)
Systematic recording; ascertainment of results; ascertainment of financial position; provision of information to users; compliance with legal requirements.

**1.5 Functions of Accounting** (6 functions)
Record-keeping; protecting business properties; communicating results; meeting legal requirements; facilitating rational decision making; providing information to management.

**1.6 Book-Keeping**
Definition: mechanical process of recording financial transactions; routine and clerical in nature.
- **1.6.1 Objectives of Book-Keeping:** Permanence of record; systematic recording; ascertainment of obligations and dues; prevention and detection of fraud.

**1.7 Distinction: Bookkeeping vs Accounting** (6-point comparison)
Scope, nature of work (routine vs. analytical), skill required, judgment, result, relationship (BK is a subset of accounting).

**1.8 Sub-Fields of Accounting** (5 sub-fields)
Financial Accounting; Management Accounting; Cost Accounting; Tax Accounting; Social Responsibility Accounting.

**1.9 Users of Accounting Information** (8 user groups)
Investors, Employees, Lenders, Suppliers & Trade Creditors, Customers, Government & Agencies, Public, Management.

**1.10 Relationship of Accounting with Other Disciplines** (5 disciplines)
Economics; Statistics; Mathematics; Law; Management.

**1.11 Limitations of Accounting** (7 limitations)
Based on historical cost; ignores price level changes; ignores qualitative aspects; based on estimates; possibility of manipulation; lack of uniformity; incomplete picture (e.g., does not record brand value).

**1.12 Role of the Accountant**
- **1.12.1 Areas of Service:** 7 service areas — accounting and auditing; financial management and reporting; taxation; management consulting; fraud investigation; information technology; international accounting
- **1.12.2 CA in Industry:** financial management, cost control, budgeting inside corporations
- **1.12.3 CA in Public Sector:** government accounting, audit, public funds management
- **1.12.4 CA in Fiscal Policies:** advice to government on tax policy, revenue, national statistics
- **1.12.5 CA and Economic Growth:** role of accountants in capital formation, investor confidence, national development

---

**Summary:**
Accounting is the systematic process of identifying, recording, classifying, summarising, interpreting, and communicating financial information for decision-making. It differs from bookkeeping in scope and analytical depth. It has multiple sub-fields, serves 8 categories of users, and has important limitations including historical cost bias. CAs serve society in 7 broad areas of practice.

### UNIT 2: Accounting Concepts, Principles and Conventions

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Understand the meaning of GAAP and distinguish concepts, principles, and conventions
- Explain all 12 generally accepted accounting principles with examples
- Identify the 3 fundamental accounting assumptions
- Describe qualitative characteristics of financial statements

**Unit Overview:** GAAP as the foundation; the 5 accountants–5 profits problem; why uniform standards matter; the 12 GAAPs with examples; 3 fundamental assumptions; qualitative characteristics of financial information.

---

**2.1 Introduction**
The "5 accountants–5 profits" problem demonstrates why accounting standards are needed. Introduction to GAAP as a set of conventions, rules, and procedures.

**2.2 Accounting Concepts**
Fundamental building blocks: broad, pervasive ideas underlying all accounting (e.g., dual aspect, going concern).

**2.3 Accounting Principles** (5 conditions principles must satisfy)
Must be objective; definite; workable; accepted by authorities; serve the purpose of financial statements.

**2.4 Accounting Conventions**
Customs or practices arising from tradition used as a guide when in doubt (e.g., conservatism, consistency).

**2.5 Overview of All 12 GAAPs** (with examples)
1. **Entity Concept** — business separate from owner (owner's personal car not in business books)
2. **Money Measurement Concept** — only monetary transactions recorded (employee morale not recorded)
3. **Periodicity Concept** — accounting done for defined periods (monthly, quarterly, annual)
4. **Accrual Concept** — revenue/expenses recognised when earned/incurred not when cash moves (electricity bill accrued even if unpaid)
5. **Matching Concept** — expenses matched with related revenues in same period (machinery rental for production)
6. **Going Concern Concept** — entity assumed to continue indefinitely (assets at cost not liquidation value)
7. **Cost Concept (Historical Cost)** — assets recorded at acquisition cost (land Rs.2,000 recorded at Rs.2,000 even if market value Rs.1L)
8. **Realisation Concept** — revenue recognised only when realised (computers Rs.20,000 recognised on sale, not order)
9. **Dual Aspect Concept** — every transaction has two aspects; Assets = Liabilities + Capital (Mr. J D example)
10. **Conservatism (Prudence)** — anticipate all losses, record no expected gains (Mr. P K Garments — inventory at lower of cost/NRV)
11. **Consistency** — same method used across periods (FIFO applied consistently)
12. **Materiality** — significant items treated strictly per GAAP; trivial items may be handled practically (pens expensed not capitalised)

**2.6 Fundamental Accounting Assumptions** (3 assumptions — AS 1 basis)
- **Going Concern:** entity continues indefinitely
- **Consistency:** same accounting policies from period to period
- **Accrual:** transactions recognised when earned/incurred

*If followed, no separate disclosure required. If not followed, fact must be disclosed.*

**2.7 Financial Statements**
Balance Sheet, Statement of P&L, Cash Flow Statement, Notes.
- **2.7.1 Qualitative Characteristics of Financial Statements** (11 characteristics):
Understandability; Relevance; Reliability; Comparability; Faithful representation; Substance over form; Neutrality; Prudence; Completeness; Timeliness; Balance between benefit and cost.

---

**Illustration 1** (Accounting Equation):
*Tests:* **Dual Aspect Concept [2.5]** — demonstrates that every transaction affects two sides; Assets = Liabilities + Equity always balances.

---

**Summary:**
GAAP provides a consistent framework. 12 principles govern recognition, measurement, and presentation. 3 fundamental accounting assumptions (going concern, consistency, accrual) form the bedrock of AS 1. Qualitative characteristics ensure information is useful to decision-makers.

### UNIT 3: Capital and Revenue Expenditures and Receipts

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Distinguish capital and revenue expenditures and receipts
- Apply the 5 considerations to classify expenditures
- Correctly classify receipts as capital or revenue

**Unit Overview:** The matching concept as foundation; time factor as key determinant; classification matrix — Capital/Revenue × Expenditure/Receipt.

---

**3.1 Introduction**
Time factor determines whether expenditure is capital or revenue. The matching concept requires correct classification to accurately compute profit.

**3.2 Considerations for Classification** (5 criteria)
(a) **Nature of business** — same item may be capital for one business, revenue for another
(b) **Recurring nature** — non-recurring → capital; recurring → revenue
(c) **Purpose of expenditure** — to acquire asset = capital; to maintain or earn revenue = revenue
(d) **Effect on revenue-generating capacity** — increases capacity = capital; maintains existing = revenue
(e) **Materiality** — large amounts treated strictly; trivial amounts may be expensed

**3.3 Capital Expenditures vs Revenue Expenditures**
- Capital expenditure: provides benefit over multiple periods; appears on Balance Sheet as asset
- Revenue expenditure: consumed in current period; charged to P&L
- **3.3.1 Key Differences Table** — Nature, Period of benefit, Balance Sheet vs P&L, Effect on profit

**3.4 Capital Receipts vs Revenue Receipts**
- Capital receipt: proceeds from sale of fixed assets, share premium, loans received; does not form part of regular income
- Revenue receipt: cash from routine trading, services, interest on deposits; credited to P&L

### UNIT 4: Contingent Assets and Contingent Liabilities

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Understand contingent asset and contingent liability definitions
- Distinguish contingent liability from a liability and from a provision

**Unit Overview:** Prudence principle as governing rule; the spectrum from contingent asset → virtually certain asset; possible obligation → contingent liability → provision → liability.

---

**4.1 Contingent Asset**
- Definition: possible asset arising from past events whose existence is confirmed only by uncertain future events not wholly within the entity's control
- Prudence: do not recognise; only disclose when inflow is probable
- Exception: when economic benefits are **virtually certain** → recognise as an asset (not contingent any more)

**4.2 Contingent Liabilities** (two-part definition)
(a) Possible obligation depending on uncertain future events (e.g., Mr. X — machine damage lawsuit pending)
(b) Present obligation that fails recognition because outflow is not probable OR cannot be measured reliably (e.g., Mr. AB — car fire; outcome unclear)

**4.3 Distinction: Contingent Liabilities vs Liabilities**
- Liability: present obligation, probable outflow, reliably measurable → recognised in financial statements
- Contingent liability: possible obligation or unquantifiable present obligation → only disclosed in notes

**4.4 Distinction: Contingent Liabilities vs Provisions** (4-point comparison table)
- Provision: present obligation; probable outflow; measurable with estimation → recognised
- Contingent liability: possible obligation or improbable outflow → only disclosed
- Example: Alpha Ltd. excise duty penalty — provision if probable; contingent liability if possible

---

**Summary:**
- Contingent asset: disclosed when probable; recognised only when virtually certain
- Contingent liability: disclosed in notes; not recognised in Balance Sheet
- Liability vs contingent liability: certainty of obligation is the key divider
- Provision vs contingent liability: probability of outflow is the divider

### UNIT 5: Accounting Policies

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Understand the meaning of accounting policies
- Apply criteria for selection of accounting policies
- Identify when accounting policies may be changed and disclose the effect

**Unit Overview:** Accounting policies as specific principles and methods chosen by management; guided by Prudence, Substance over Form, and Materiality; criteria for change.

---

**5.1 Meaning of Accounting Policies**
Specific accounting principles and methods of applying them. Areas include (illustrative, not exhaustive):
- Valuation of inventories (FIFO / weighted average method)
- Valuation of investments
- Treatment of depreciation

**5.2 Selection of Accounting Policies** (3 criteria)
(a) **Prudence** — degree of caution in uncertain conditions (inventory at lower of cost or NRV)
(b) **Substance over form** — economic reality over legal form (lease assets capitalised)
(c) **Materiality** — insignificant items need not receive strict treatment; significant items must be disclosed

Examples: investments at acquisition cost; inventory at lower of cost / NRV.

**5.3 Change in Accounting Policies** (2 conditions for permissible change)
1. Required by statute or an Accounting Standard
2. Change results in more appropriate presentation of financial statements

*Examples:* Change from weighted average to FIFO method; capitalisation of borrowing costs.
*Omega Enterprises example:* change in inventory valuation method with proper disclosure and effect.

---

**Summary:**
- Accounting policies are specific principles and methods; not a single fixed list
- Selection must be guided by Prudence, Substance over Form, and Materiality
- Policies can only be changed for specific reasons; effect of change must be disclosed

### UNIT 6: Accounting as a Measurement Discipline — Valuation Principles, Accounting Estimates

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Understand what measurement means in accounting (3 elements)
- Identify objects/events to be measured and the scale of measurement
- Explain 4 valuation bases with numerical examples
- Distinguish measurement from valuation
- Understand accounting estimates and their role

**Unit Overview:** 3 elements of measurement; 4 valuation bases; limitations of money as a measurement scale; the difference between valuation and estimation.

---

**6.1 Meaning of Measurement**
- Chambers' definition: assignment of numerals to objects or events according to rules
- Kohler's definition: process of determining monetary amounts for financial elements
- **3 elements of measurement:** (i) Identification of the object/event; (ii) Selection of a standard or scale; (iii) Evaluation of the dimension of the object/event

**6.2 Objects or Events to be Measured**
Past (recorded transactions), present (current value of assets/liabilities), and future (estimates). Used in: cash management decisions, loan decisions.

**6.3 Standard or Scale of Measurement**
Money is the common scale in accounting. But no universal denomination; exchange rate fluctuations complicate measurement (USD Rs.50 → Rs.55 example).

**6.4 Dimension of Measurement Scale**
Ideal scale should be stable over time. Money is unstable due to inflation. Example: production 5,000 pcs for Rs.5L vs 4,500 pcs for Rs.5.4L → 8% monetary growth but 10% physical decline.

**6.5 Accounting as a Measurement Discipline**
Not a purely exact measurement discipline because of estimates, assumptions. Relies on fundamental assumptions: going concern, consistency, accrual.

**6.6 Valuation Principles** (4 bases — with numerical examples)
1. **Historical Cost:** original acquisition cost. Machine Rs.7L + Rs.1L installation = Rs.8L. Loan Rs.5L at 10% recorded at Rs.5L. Income tax liability at expected amount.
2. **Current Cost:** replacement cost at current date. Machine originally Rs.7L, current price Rs.25L. Bank loan settled today = Rs.5,05,000.
3. **Realisable Value:** net selling price on orderly disposal. Machine would sell for Rs.20L. Inventories at lower of cost/NRV.
4. **Present Value:** discounted future cash flows. Machine generates Rs.1L p.a. for 10 years @ 20%; PV = Rs.4,19,246. Bank loan PV = Rs.4,70,094.

*Summary comparison table (Historical / Current / Realisable / Present) for both machine and loan.*

**6.7 Measurement and Valuation**
Money as a monetary surrogate. Ordinal vs cardinal scales. Accounting is closer to a valuation (not pure measurement) discipline.

**6.8 Accounting Estimates**
Items that cannot be exactly measured using valuation principles; require professional judgment:
- Patent Rs.10L — estimated useful life
- Construction contract — % completion method
- Tax provision — estimated liability
- Company XY machine Rs.10L — useful life changed from 10 years to 3 years (change in estimate, prospective treatment)

---

**Summary:**
- Measurement involves 3 elements: identification, scale selection, evaluation
- Money is the scale but is unstable due to inflation
- 4 valuation bases: historical cost (most common), current cost, realisable value, present value
- Accounting estimates are necessary where exact measurement is not possible; changes in estimates are applied prospectively

### UNIT 7: Accounting Standards

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Understand what accounting standards are and why they are needed
- Know the benefits and limitations of AS
- Describe the 8-step AS formulation process
- Identify the 3 sets of standards applicable in India and the list of 27 effective AS

**Unit Overview:** 4 issues AS address; harmonisation; the ASB; 8-step process; 3 sets of standards in India (Ind AS, AS under Companies Act, ICAI AS).

---

**7.1 Introduction**
Accounting as the "language of business." 4 issues AS deal with: (i) Recognition of events and transactions; (ii) Measurement; (iii) Presentation; (iv) Disclosure requirements.

**7.2 Objectives of Accounting Standards** (2 objectives)
1. Eliminate non-comparability of financial statements through harmonisation of diverse accounting policies
2. Provide standard accounting policies, valuation norms, and disclosure requirements

**7.3 Benefits and Limitations**
- **Benefits (3):**
  (i) Standardise alternative accounting treatments; reduce diversity
  (ii) Mandate additional disclosures beyond statutory law
  (iii) Facilitate comparability — domestic and international
- **Limitations (2):**
  (i) Difficulty choosing between alternative treatments when multiple approaches permitted
  (ii) Restricted scope — cannot override applicable statutes and laws

**7.4 Process of Formulation of AS**
- ASB constituted in 1977 by ICAI
- IFRS considered in formulation
- ASB composition: ASSOCHAM, CII, FICCI, regulators (SEBI, RBI, NFRA), academicians
- **8-step process:**
  Step 1: Identification of topics
  Step 2: Preparation of point outline
  Step 3: Study of IFRS and other national standards
  Step 4: Preparation of exposure draft (ED)
  Step 5: Circulation of ED to members and other specified bodies
  Step 6: Discussion at open meetings
  Step 7: Finalisation of AS considering comments
  Step 8: Issue of AS by ICAI Council under authority of MCA for companies
- **3 sets of AS in India:**
  1. **Ind AS** — for listed companies, NBFCs with net worth ≥ Rs.250cr
  2. **AS under Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules 2021** — for unlisted companies
  3. **ICAI AS** — for non-companies (partnerships, proprietorships)

**7.5 List of AS in India**
- 29 issued; **27 currently effective** (AS 6 withdrawn on revision of AS 10 in 2016; AS 8 withdrawn on issuance of AS 26)
- AS 1 to AS 29 (with AS 6 and AS 8 withdrawn)
- **Ind AS list** (as on 1.8.2024): Ind AS 101–116, Ind AS 1–2, 7–8, 10, 12, 16, 19–21, 23–24, 27–29, 32–34, 36–38, 40–41
- **ASLB (Accounting Standards for Local Bodies):** issued by ICAI for panchayats, municipalities

---

**Summary:**
- AS are written policy documents covering recognition, measurement, presentation, and disclosure
- 27 AS currently effective in India (AS 6 and AS 8 withdrawn)
- ASB formulates AS through an 8-step process with public consultation
- 3 sets of standards apply to different entity types in India

### CHAPTER 1: Introduction to Accounting Standards

**Learning Outcomes** (9 outcomes):
1. Understand GAAP and the meaning of accounting standards
2. Describe the standards-setting process
3. Know how many AS exist and their status
4. Understand need for convergence with global standards
5. Explain the role of IASB and IFRS
6. Distinguish adoption from convergence
7. Understand benefits of IFRS compliance
8. Explain carve outs and carve ins in Ind AS
9. Know the Ind AS roadmap for different entity categories

**Chapter Overview:** AS as a tool for harmonisation → need for global convergence → IASB/IFRS → India's choice of convergence (not adoption) → Ind AS → roadmap for phased implementation.

---

**Section 1: Introduction**
- GAAP: conventions, rules, procedures guiding financial reporting
- AS definition (MCA/NFRA): written policy documents covering recognition, measurement, presentation, disclosure
- **4 issues AS deal with:** recognition, measurement, presentation, disclosure
- **3 Benefits:** standardise alternative treatments; additional disclosures; domestic and international comparability

**Section 2: Standards Setting Process**
- ASB constituted 1977 by ICAI
- ASIs (Accounting Standard Interpretations) merged into respective AS as 'Explanations'
- Section 133 of Companies Act 2013: Central Government may prescribe AS as recommended by ICAI in consultation with NFRA
- **8-step formulation process (Step I–VIII):**
  - Step I: Identification of topic
  - Step II: Preparation of point outline
  - Step III: Study of IFRS / other national standards
  - Step IV: Preparation of Exposure Draft (ED)
  - Step V: Circulation of ED to members and specified bodies
  - Step VI: Open meeting discussions
  - Step VII: Finalisation considering comments
  - Step VIII: Issue of AS by ICAI Council

**Section 3: How Many Accounting Standards?**
- 29 issued; **27 currently effective**
- AS 6 (Depreciation Accounting) withdrawn on revision of AS 10 (PPE) in 2016
- AS 8 (R&D) withdrawn on issuance of AS 26 (Intangible Assets)
- **Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules 2021** — operative from 1.4.2021 (replaced 2006 Rules)
- List of AS 1 to AS 29 with dates of applicability
- Study material coverage: Chapters 4–10 cover specific AS

**Section 4: Status of Accounting Standards**
- ASB cannot override applicable laws and statutes
- Mandatory from dates notified by MCA/ICAI
- 27 AS notified under Companies (AS) Rules 2021
- Implication: ICAI members must examine compliance; disclose deviations in audit report

**Section 5: Need for Convergence Towards Global Standards** (4 reasons)
1. Raising funds internationally / overseas acquisitions
2. Comparability of financial statements across borders
3. Uniformity and transparency in reporting
4. Facilitating global investment flows; avoids reinstatement of financial statements

**Section 6: International Accounting Standard Board (IASB)**
- **IASC** established June 1973 (London-based); professional accountancy bodies of 75+ countries including ICAI
- Issued International Accounting Standards (IAS) from 1973–2001
- Restructured 1997–1999 → **IASB** effective 1st April 2001
- IASB publishes **IFRS** (International Financial Reporting Standards)
- Pre-2001 standards: IAS (by IASC); post-2001: IFRS (by IASB); both continue to be in use

**Section 7: International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) as Global Standards**
**4 components of IFRS:**
1. IFRS issued by IASB
2. IAS issued by IASC (adopted by IASB)
3. Interpretations issued by SIC (Standards Interpretations Committee)
4. Interpretations issued by IFRIC (IFRS Interpretations Committee)

IFRS are **principles-based** standards — broad rules, not prescriptive treatments. Major nations moving toward adoption for stock exchange listing.

**Section 8: Becoming IFRS Compliant (Adoption vs Convergence)**
- **Technique I — Adoption:** Country sets specific timetable to use IFRS exactly as issued by IASB
- **Technique II — Convergence:** Country develops compatible high-quality standards aligned with IFRS, with local adaptations

**Benefits of Convergence (5):**
1. Cross-border flow of money
2. Helps investors in decision making
3. Listing of companies at global stock exchanges
4. Comparability of financial statements
5. Low risk of error; greater transparency

**Section 9: Carve Outs / Carve Ins in Ind AS**
India chose **convergence** (not adoption) after detailed analysis and stakeholder consultation.

**3 types of departures from IFRS in Ind AS:**
1. **Terminology changes** — 'Statement of P&L' instead of 'Statement of Comprehensive Income'; 'Balance Sheet' instead of 'Statement of Financial Position'
2. **Removal of options** — removes IFRS choices to ensure consistency; these do **not** result in carve outs
3. **Differences due to Indian economic environment** — deviations from IFRS principles; these **are carve outs**

- **Carve Out:** Deviation from accounting principles stated in IFRS (due to different Indian economic conditions)
- **Carve In:** Additional guidance given in Ind AS over and above IFRS

**Section 10: Convergence to IFRS in India**
- ICAI is a member of IFAC (International Federation of Accountants)
- Aim: comply with IFRS to the extent possible
- Overseas acquisitions by Indian companies create demand for high-quality standards
- Decision: **two separate sets of standards:**
  (i) Ind AS (IFRS-converged) — for Ind AS entities
  (ii) Existing AS — for non-Ind AS entities

**Section 11: What are Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS)?**
- IFRS-converged standards issued by Central Government of India
- Issued under supervision and control of ASB of ICAI; in consultation with NFRA
- NFRA recommends to MCA; MCA prescribes for companies
- Numbered same as IAS; for IFRS-based Ind AS: IFRS number + 100 (e.g., IFRS 1 → Ind AS 101)

**Section 12: History of IFRS-converged Ind AS**
- 2006: ICAI initiated move towards IFRS
- Original roadmap: implementation from 2011 — postponed due to unresolved tax issues
- **July 2014:** Finance Minister announced urgency to converge with IFRS
- **February 16, 2015:** MCA issued Companies (Indian Accounting Standards) Rules 2015 — revised roadmap for companies (excluding banks, NBFCs, insurance companies)

**Section 13: List of Ind AS**
Comprehensive table mapping Ind AS (101–116, 1–2, 7–8, 10, 12, 16, 19–21, 23–24, 27–29, 32–34, 36–38, 40–41) to corresponding IAS/IFRS and existing AS.
Key mappings: Ind AS 101 = IFRS 1; Ind AS 110 = IFRS 10 = AS 21 (Consolidated FS); Ind AS 115 = IFRS 15 = AS 7 + AS 9; Ind AS 116 = IFRS 16 = AS 19.

**Section 14: Roadmap for Implementation of Ind AS**

*For Companies (excluding Banks, NBFCs, Insurance):*
- **Phase I:** 1.4.2015 (voluntary); 1.4.2016 (mandatory) — Companies listed/in process of listing with net worth ≥ Rs.500cr; unlisted companies with net worth ≥ Rs.500cr; their parent, subsidiary, associate, JV
- **Phase II:** 1.4.2017 (mandatory) — all other listed/to-be-listed companies (except SME exchanges); unlisted with net worth ≥ Rs.250cr but < Rs.500cr; their parent, subsidiary, associate, JV

*Special Points:*
- Companies listed on SME exchange: not required to apply Ind AS; continue with existing AS
- Once Ind AS adopted — no going back; apply in all subsequent financial statements
- Companies not covered by roadmap: continue with AS under Companies (AS) Rules 2006

*For NBFCs:*
- **Phase I:** 1.4.2018 — NBFCs (listed or unlisted) with net worth ≥ Rs.500cr; their holding/subsidiary/JV/associate not already covered
- **Phase II:** 1.4.2019 — NBFCs with listed equity/debt securities with net worth < Rs.500cr; unlisted NBFCs with net worth ≥ Rs.250cr but < Rs.500cr; their subsidiaries/JVs/associates
- NBFCs with net worth < Rs.250cr and not otherwise covered: continue with AS under Companies (AS) Rules 2006
- Voluntary adoption NOT allowed for NBFCs

*For Scheduled Commercial Banks (SCBs, excluding RRBs):*
- Initially 1.4.2018; RBI deferred to 1.4.2019; RBI notification 22.3.2019 — further deferred **till further notice**
- UCBs and RRBs: not required to apply Ind AS

*For Insurers / Insurance Companies:*
- MCA roadmap: 1.4.2018; IRDAI deferred to 1.4.2020; IRDAI circular 21.1.2020 — deferred **till further notice**

---

**Summary:**
- AS aim to improve quality of financial reporting through comparability, consistency, and transparency
- ICAI issued 29 AS; 27 currently effective (AS 6 and AS 8 withdrawn)
- India chose convergence (not adoption) of IFRS → Ind AS
- Ind AS are IFRS-converged standards; MCA notification of 16.2.2015 gave roadmap
- Separate roadmaps for companies, NBFCs, banks, and insurance companies

### CHAPTER 2: Framework for Preparation and Presentation of Financial Statements

**Learning Outcomes** (5 outcomes):
1. Understand meaning and significance of the Framework
2. Learn objectives of financial statements
3. Understand qualitative characteristics of financial statements
4. Comprehend recognition and measurement of elements
5. Know concepts of capital, capital maintenance, and determination of profit

**Chapter Overview:** ASB issued Framework (July 2000) — sets out 8 principal areas covering the conceptual basis for financial statements; relevant for entities following Companies (AS) Rules 2021.

---

**Section 1: Introduction**
- Framework issued by ASB of ICAI in July 2000
- Provides fundamental basis for development and review of AS
- **8 principal areas covered:**
  (a) Components of financial statements
  (b) Objectives of financial statements
  (c) Assumptions underlying financial statements
  (d) Qualitative characteristics of financial statements
  (e) Elements of financial statements
  (f) Criteria for recognition of elements
  (g) Principles for measurement of financial elements
  (h) Concepts of capital and capital maintenance

**Section 2: Purpose of the Framework**
The framework assists (6 parties):
(a) Enterprises in preparation of FS — especially for topics not covered by any AS
(b) ASB in development and review of AS
(c) ASB in promoting harmonisation; reducing alternative treatments
(d) Auditors in forming opinion on whether FS conform to AS
(e) Users in interpreting FS
(f) Those interested in ASB's work

**Section 3: Status and Scope of the Framework**
- Applies to general-purpose FS prepared annually for external users by all commercial, industrial, and business enterprises (public or private sector)
- Special-purpose reports (e.g., tax computation): outside scope, but framework may be applied to the extent consistent
- **Key rule:** Framework does NOT override any specific AS — in case of conflict, AS prevails over Framework

**Section 4: Components of Financial Statements**
Complete set of FS consists of:
1. **Balance Sheet** — value of economic resources controlled; liquidity and solvency
2. **Statement of Profit and Loss** — results of operations; profitability
3. **Cash Flow Statement** — how enterprise generated and used cash
4. **Notes and other statements** — supplementary information (accounting policies, segment reporting, related party disclosures, EPS, etc.)

All components are interrelated; none in isolation tells the complete story.

**Section 5: Objectives and Users of Financial Statements**
- **Objective:** Provide information about financial position, performance, and cash flows useful to a wide range of users in making economic decisions
- **7 broad groups of users:**
  (a) Investors — risk, return, ability to pay dividends
  (b) Employees — stability, profitability, remuneration, retirement benefits
  (c) Lenders — whether loans and interest will be paid when due
  (d) Suppliers and trade creditors — whether amounts owing will be paid
  (e) Customers — continuance of enterprise for long-term supply
  (f) Governments and agencies — allocation of resources; tax policies; national income statistics
  (g) Public — enterprise's contribution to local economy; employment; trends

**Section 6: Fundamental Accounting Assumptions** (3 assumptions)
If followed, no separate disclosure required; if not followed, must be specifically disclosed.
- **(a) Going Concern:** Enterprise continues in operation in the foreseeable future; no intention to liquidate or curtail operations materially
- **(b) Accrual:** Revenue and costs recognised as earned/incurred, not as cash is received/paid (Section 128(1) of Companies Act 2013 — mandatory for companies)
- **(c) Consistency:** Accounting policies consistent from one period to another; can be changed only if required by statute, AS, or for more appropriate presentation

---

**Illustration 1** (Trader balance sheet 31.3.20X1):
*Tests:* **Going Concern assumption [Section 6(a)]** — prepares full P&L and Balance Sheet under (i) going concern and (ii) not going concern; demonstrates how asset values, depreciation, provisions, and equity differ depending on whether the going concern assumption is applied.

**Example 1** (Cash vs Accrual basis P&L — Article A and B):
*Tests:* **Accrual vs Cash basis [Section 6(b)]** — same transactions produce radically different profits in Period 1 and Period 2 under cash basis (Period 1: profit Rs.58,000; Period 2: loss Rs.47,500); accrual basis correctly shows profit in Period 1 = Rs.10,500, Period 2 = nil. Demonstrates irrationality of cash basis.

---

**Section 7: Qualitative Characteristics of Financial Statements**
**4 qualitative characteristics** (within limits of reasonable cost/benefit):
1. **Understandability** — information readily understandable by users with reasonable knowledge of business and accounting
2. **Relevance** — information that influences economic decisions; related to materiality (materiality is a threshold, not a primary characteristic)
3. **Reliability** — free from material error and bias; sub-characteristics: (a) Faithful representation; (b) Substance over form; (c) Neutrality; (d) Prudence; (e) Completeness
4. **Comparability** — permits inter-firm and intra-firm comparison; requires disclosure of effect of accounting policy changes

**Constraints on Relevant and Reliable information:**
- **(a) Timeliness** — delay reduces relevance; balance between timeliness and reliability
- **(b) Balance between Benefit and Cost** — pervasive constraint; benefit of information should exceed its cost

**Section 8: True and Fair View**
- FS required to give true and fair view of performance, financial position, and cash flows
- Framework does not directly define TFV but application of qualitative characteristics and AS normally results in TFV
- Auditors confirm compliance with AS → TFV

**Section 9: Elements of Financial Statements**
Framework classifies FS items into **5 elements:**
1. **Asset:** Resource controlled by the enterprise as a result of past events from which future economic benefits are expected to flow
   - Sub-points: (a) Need not have physical substance (patents, trade receivables are assets); (b) Control not ownership (finance lease — lessee records asset); (c) Insufficient control → not an asset (e.g., specific employee talent); (d) Must be probable that future economic benefits flow; (e) Must have reliably measurable cost or value; (f) When benefit beyond current period is improbable → expense, not asset
2. **Liability:** Present obligation arising from past events, settlement of which is expected to result in an outflow of resources embodying economic benefits
   - Sub-points: (a) Must be probable present obligation; provision = liability measurable by estimation; (b) Provisions for doubtful debts, depreciation, impairment = diminution in asset value, not liabilities; (c) Obligation normally arises on delivery or irrevocable agreement, not mere management decision
3. **Equity:** Residual interest in the assets of an enterprise after deducting all liabilities (A – L = E). Items: paid-up capital + reserves. Value changes through capital contributions/distributions and through income/expenses.
4. **Income:** Increase in economic benefits during the period in the form of inflows or enhancements of assets or decreases in liabilities that result in increase in equity other than those relating to contributions from equity participants. Encompasses **Revenue** (ordinary course of activity) and **Gains** (may or may not be in ordinary course).
5. **Expenses:** Decrease in economic benefits during the period in the form of outflows or depletions of assets or incurrence of liabilities that result in decrease in equity other than those relating to distributions to equity participants. Encompasses routine expenses and **Losses**.

*Gains and losses are not recognised as separate elements — their economic characteristics are same as income and expenses.*

Key equation: **Profit = CE – OE – C + D = (CA – CL) – (OA – OL) – C + D**

---

**Example 2** (A Ltd. binding agreement with P Ltd. — custom machine Rs.40,000):
*Tests:* **Liability recognition criteria [Section 9.2]** — irrevocable binding contract → recognise liability of Rs.40,000; machine has no future economic benefit (scrapped) → entire Rs.40,000 recognised as expense; Journal entry: Loss on change in production method Dr. To P Ltd.

**Example 3** (Trader — transactions affecting A–L=E):
*Tests:* **Income definition and A–L=E relationship [Section 9.4]** — capital introduced Rs.20,000; income from investment Rs.8,000; liability settled at discount Rs.1,000 gain — shows income increases equity without capital introduction; A–L=E holds after each transaction.

**Example 4** (Trader — wages, rent outstanding, drawings):
*Tests:* **Expense definition and A–L=E [Section 9.5]** — wages reduce assets; outstanding rent increases liability; drawings reduce equity; expenses decrease equity without drawings; A–L=E holds at all points.

---

**Section 10: Measurement of Elements of Financial Statements**
Measurement = process of determining money value at which an element is recognised in FS.
Framework recognises **4 measurement bases:**
1. **Historical Cost:** Acquisition price. Assets at amount of cash paid or fair value at time of acquisition. Liabilities at proceeds received for obligation. Most common basis in practice.
2. **Current Cost:** Amount required to acquire same/equivalent asset currently (assets); undiscounted amount required to settle obligation currently (liabilities). Example: machine acquired for $10,000 deferred; current market value = Rs.5L (current cost).
3. **Realisable (Settlement) Value:** For assets: current cash realisable on sale in orderly disposal. For liabilities: undiscounted amount expected to be paid on settlement in normal course.
4. **Present Value:** Assets at present value of future net cash inflows expected in normal course. Liabilities at present value of future net cash outflows required to settle. Present value formula: P = A × [1/(1+R)^n].

AS largely uses historical cost; for some items other bases permitted (e.g., inventories at lower of cost/NRV = combination of historical cost + realisable value).

---

**Example 5** (Mr. X — machine Rs.7L; historical cost):
*Tests:* **Historical cost — asset and liability [Section 10.1]** — machine: historical cost = Rs.7L (acquisition price); loan Rs.5L at 18% — recorded at Rs.5L (proceeds received).

**Example 6** (Machine $10,000 deferred payment; rate Rs.49/$; current price Rs.5L):
*Tests:* **Current cost — asset and liability [Section 10.2]** — current cost of machine = Rs.5L; liability at current cost = Rs.4,90,000 (undiscounted; not present value).

**Example 7** (Machine Rs.40,000; generates Rs.10,000/year for 5 years; NRV Rs.35,000; rate 10%):
*Tests:* **Present value — value in use [Section 10.4]** — value in use = Rs.37,900 (PV of Rs.10,000 × discount factors 0.909+0.826+0.751+0.683+0.621); recoverable value = Rs.37,900; carrying amount Rs.40,000 is overstated by Rs.2,100.

---

**Section 11: Capital Maintenance**
Capital = net assets of a business. Purpose: ensure retained profit is not negative; business can continue at same activity level.

**3 concepts of capital maintenance:**
1. **Financial Capital Maintenance at Historical Cost:** Opening and closing assets stated at respective historical costs. Retained Profit = CE – OE – C (adjusted for drawings). Positive → capital maintained at historical cost.
2. **Financial Capital Maintenance at Current Purchasing Power:** Opening equity restated at closing prices using average price indices. Positive retained profit → business can replace assets at average closing prices.
3. **Physical Capital Maintenance at Current Costs:** Historical costs of opening and closing assets restated using specific price indices applicable to each asset. Positive retained profit → business can replace each specific asset at its current cost.

---

**Example 8** (Trader: 6,000 units at Rs.2; sells at Rs.3; withdraws Rs.6,000):
*Tests:* **Financial capital maintenance at historical cost [Section 11]** — Retained Profit = nil (Rs.12,000 – Rs.12,000); business can continue at same level if no price change.

**Example 9** (Same trader; price index rises 100→120):
*Tests:* **Financial capital maintenance at current purchasing power [Section 11]** — Opening equity restated = Rs.14,400; Retained Profit = Rs.12,000 – Rs.14,400 = (–) Rs.2,400; should have restricted drawings to Rs.3,600.

**Illustration 2** (Trader: 6,000 units at Rs.2; specific price rises to Rs.2.50):
*Tests:* **All three capital maintenance concepts [Section 11]** — (i) Historical cost: Retained Profit = nil; (ii) Current purchasing power (index 100→120): Retained Profit = (–) Rs.3,000; (iii) Physical: Rs.12,000 – Rs.15,000 = (–) Rs.3,000. Shows different conclusions under each method.

---

**Summary:**
- **Components:** BS (financial position), P&L (performance), Cash Flow (liquidity), Notes (supplementary)
- **7 Users and their needs** (table)
- **3 Fundamental Assumptions:** Accrual (recognised when earned), Going Concern (continue indefinitely), Consistency (same policies across periods)
- **4 Qualitative Characteristics:** Understandability, Relevance, Reliability, Comparability
- **5 Elements** with definitions: Asset, Liability, Equity, Income/Gain, Expense/Loss
- **4 Measurement Bases:** Historical Cost (most common), Current Cost, Realisable Value, Present Value
- **3 Capital Maintenance concepts:** Financial at historical cost; Financial at current purchasing power; Physical at current costs

### CHAPTER 3: Applicability of Accounting Standards

**Learning Outcomes** (2 outcomes):
1. Comprehend the status of Accounting Standards
2. Understand the applicability of Accounting Standards to different entity types

**Chapter Overview:** Status of AS → 3-question applicability test → corporate entities (SMCs vs Non-SMCs) → non-corporate entities (MSMEs vs Large) → ICDS.

---

**Section 1: Status of Accounting Standards**

**3-Question Applicability Test:**
(a) Does the AS apply to the enterprise concerned?
(b) Does it apply to the financial statement concerned?
(c) Does it apply to the financial item concerned?

**Enterprises covered:**
- Any enterprise engaged in commercial, industrial, or business activities, whether or not profit-oriented and even if charitable/religious
- **Exception:** enterprises solely conducting non-commercial activities (e.g., pure charitable donation collection) are excluded
- If any part of activity is commercial — AS applies to ALL activities

**Implication of Mandatory Status:**
- Where statute requires compliance (e.g., Companies Act): mandatory
- Where statute does not require (e.g., partnership firm): ICAI members must examine compliance during attest functions; report deviations in audit report
- **Section 129(1) Companies Act 2013:** companies must present FS in accordance with AS notified under Section 133
- **Section 143(3)(e):** auditor must report whether FS comply with AS under Section 133
- **Section 129(5):** if FS do not comply → disclose deviation, reason, and financial effect

**Exceptions under Section 129(5):**
Non-disclosure does NOT mean FS do not give true and fair view if the matter is not required to be disclosed under:
(a) Insurance Act 1938 / IRDAI Act 1999 (insurance companies)
(b) Banking Regulation Act 1949 (banking companies)
(c) Electricity Act 2003 (electricity companies)
(d) Any other applicable law

**Financial Items to Which AS Apply:**
- Only **material items** — item is material if its omission or misstatement is likely to affect economic decisions
- Materiality is not function of size alone; depends on information content (e.g., penalty of Rs.50,000 paid by a large company for breach of law = material despite small amount)
- Material items must be shown separately (e.g., penalties should not be clubbed with legal charges)

**Accounting Standards and Income Tax Act 1961:**
- AS reduce diversity in accounting; IT Act governs deductions/exemptions based on fiscal policy
- These are separate — an expense required by AS in P&L does not mean it is deductible under Income Tax
- Example: Depreciation on finance lease assets → lessee charges in books (AS 19) but tax depreciation allowed to lessor

**Income Computation and Disclosure Standards (ICDS):**
- Under Section 145(2) of IT Act 1961 — Central Government notified **10 ICDS** for assessees (other than individual/HUF not required to get accounts audited u/s 44AB) following mercantile system; applicable from AY 2017-18
- **10 notified ICDS:**
  - ICDS I: Accounting Policies
  - ICDS II: Valuation of Inventories
  - ICDS III: Construction Contracts
  - ICDS IV: Revenue Recognition
  - ICDS V: Tangible Fixed Assets
  - ICDS VI: Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates
  - ICDS VII: Government Grants
  - ICDS VIII: Securities
  - ICDS IX: Borrowing Costs
  - ICDS X: Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets

---

**Section 2: Applicability of Accounting Standards**

**2.1 Revised Criteria for Non-Company Entities** (ICAI Council, 433rd meeting, August 13–15, 2024; effective from accounting periods commencing on or after 1.4.2024)

**Two categories of non-company entities:**

**MSME (Micro, Small and Medium Sized Entity)** — must satisfy ALL 5 conditions:
(i) Equity or debt securities NOT listed / not in process of listing on any stock exchange (India or outside)
(ii) NOT a bank, financial institution, or insurance company
(iii) Turnover (excluding other income) does NOT exceed Rs.250 crore in the immediately preceding accounting year
(iv) Borrowings do NOT exceed Rs.50 crore at any time during the immediately preceding accounting year
(v) NOT a holding or subsidiary of an entity that is not an MSME

**Large Entity** = non-company entity that does NOT satisfy one or more of the above 5 conditions.

**Additional Requirements for MSMEs availing exemptions:**
1. Must disclose (in notes) that it is an MSME and has complied with AS insofar as applicable
2. If MSME loses exemption qualification — full AS applies from current period; no need to revise prior year; disclose the fact
3. An entity that was not an MSME and becomes MSME — cannot avail exemptions until it remains MSME for **two consecutive years**
4. If MSME opts out of some (not all) exemptions — must disclose which standards it has availed exemption from
5. If MSME opts out of all exemptions — must comply fully
6. Partial exemption/relaxation must not mislead users

**Annexure 1: Applicability of AS to Non-Company Entities**
*(All 27 AS listed — AS 1 to AS 29, effective as on 1.4.2024)*

**Large entities:** full compliance with all 27 AS

**MSMEs — AS NOT applicable in their entirety:**
*(A)(i) Not applicable to ALL MSMEs:*
- AS 3: Cash Flow Statements
- AS 17: Segment Reporting
- AS 20: Earnings per Share
- AS 24: Discontinuing Operations

*(A)(ii) AS 18 (Related Party Disclosures) and AS 28 (Impairment of Assets) NOT applicable to MSMEs with:*
- Turnover ≤ Rs.50 crore AND
- Borrowings ≤ Rs.10 crore AND
- Not a holding/subsidiary of MSME not covered above

*(B) Relaxations from certain requirements for ALL MSMEs:*
- AS 10: May not comply with para 87 (encouraged disclosures on PPE)
- AS 11: May not comply with para 44 (encouraged disclosures on FX rates)
- AS 15: May not comply with paras 11–16 (non-vesting short-term compensated absences); paras 46/139 (discounting); paras 50–116 and 117–123 (defined benefit plans — can use rational method instead of actuarial); paras 129–131 (other long-term benefits)
- AS 19: May not comply with specific disclosure paragraphs (22(c)/(e)/(f); 25(a)/(b)/(e); 37(a)/(f)/(g); 38; 46(b)/(d)/(e))
- AS 22: Must comply for current tax; exempt from deferred tax provisions
- AS 26: May not comply with paras 90(d)(iii), 90(d)(iv), 98 (disclosures on intangibles)
- AS 28: May measure 'value in use' by reasonable estimate (not present value technique); certain disclosure paras not required
- AS 29: May not comply with paras 66 and 67 (disclosures on provisions/contingent liabilities)

*(C) Generally no MSME transactions for:* AS 14 (Amalgamations); AS 27 (JV — jointly controlled operations/assets) — not applicable unless MSME actually has such transactions

*(D) AS 21, AS 23, AS 27 (consolidated FS requirements), AS 25 (Interim FR):* Do not require non-company entity to present consolidated FS or interim report. Applicable only if entity is required or elects to do so.

**2.3 Applicability to Companies (Not Following Ind AS)**

**2.3.1 AS Applicable in Their Entirety to Non-SMC Companies (17 AS):**
AS 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18, 21, 22, 23, 24, 26, 27

**2.3.2 Exemptions/Relaxations for Small and Medium-Sized Companies (SMCs)** as per MCA Notification dated June 23, 2021:
*(1) AS Not Applicable to SMCs in entirety:*
- AS 17: Segment Reporting (not applicable to SMCs)

*(2) Relaxations for SMCs:*
- AS 15 (Employee Benefits): same relaxations as MSMEs above; however, SMCs must actuarially determine defined benefit liability using PUC Method with government bond yield as discount rate
- AS 19 (Leases): specific disclosure paragraphs not applicable to SMCs
- AS 20 (EPS): diluted EPS exempted for SMCs
- AS 28 (Impairment): may use reasonable estimate for 'value in use' (not PV technique)
- AS 29 (Provisions): paras 66 and 67 not applicable

*(3) AS 25 (Interim FR):* Does not require a company to present interim FS. Applicable only when required by regulators (e.g., SEBI requires quarterly results); recognition/measurement requirements apply to Non-SMCs for interim FS.

**Summary:**
- For companies: two levels — **SMCs** (with exemptions) and **Non-SMCs** (full compliance)
- For non-company entities: two levels — **MSMEs** (with exemptions, effective 1.4.2024) and **Large entities** (full compliance)
- MSMEs must have turnover ≤ Rs.250cr, borrowings ≤ Rs.50cr, not listed, not bank/FI/insurance, not holding/subsidiary of non-MSME
