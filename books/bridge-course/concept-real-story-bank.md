## The Walk Out

The last paper always feels different when you know what went wrong.

Everyone else was walking out in groups -laughing, comparing answers, already
planning where to eat. Arjun walked alone. Head down. Bag strap held tight
across one shoulder like he was carrying something heavier than books.

He didn't want to talk to anyone.

Not because he was angry at them. He just had nothing to say. *How did it go?
You okay?* The questions would come and he would have to answer them. He
couldn't do that right now. So he turned left out of the gate and started
walking toward the bus stand.

His mind wouldn't stop. Each scene just keeps flashing one after the other

*Class six.* Teacher going around the room asking each student -what do you want to become? Doctor.
Engineer. Collector. IAS. When the teacher asked him he had said -*A successful person.*
A few students laughed. The teacher smiled gently and moved on. Arjun hadn't minded. Arjun has actually meant it.

*Class nine.* Rajan uncle, his neighbour two houses down, suddenly collapsed one afternoon. Everyone
panicked and worried . The doctor who stepped out of the ambulance -calm, certain,
knowing exactly what to do , the doctor brought everyone out of that fear. The doctor had not only cured Rajan Uncle but had done something to Arjun ( watching from his balcony) that he still couldn't
explain properly. That night Arjun told his mother: I want to become a doctor. She
said: then study. He studied.

*Class eleven.* A video going around in the class group. A hospital
inauguration. A man speaking about building something bigger than himself.
Naresh Trehan. Medanta. Arjun watched it four times. Something shifted. Not
just *I want to help people* -something bigger. *I want to do something for
this country.* He felt slightly embarrassed thinking it. It was the kind of
thing people said in speeches. But he had felt it for real.

Then class twelve results. And the silence at home that nobody spoke about but
everyone felt. The kind that sits at the dinner table and doesn't leave.

He had seen his father's face when the private medical college fee came on
screen. Just one second. His father looked away quickly. But Arjun had already
seen it.

He didn't apply.

---

He had found the CA idea almost by accident. A friend who had gotten 2 subjects
back in 12th, whose family was pushing him toward CA. Arjun had looked
it up that night. Then found a video -PM Modi speaking at some function to a
room full of chartered accountants.

There was one line that made him stop scrolling.

*Accountants are partners in nation building.*

That same feeling from the Medanta video came back. And Pranav Bhaiya (his relative cum mentor) was a CA.
Had been saying for years that CA was a serious profession, a real path. Arjun
had trusted that.

He had enrolled.

And now here he was. Walking from the exam center to the bus stand after a paper that had taken
something out of him. The entries he had practiced had disappeared under
pressure. He had sat staring at one question for twenty minutes and couldn't
begin.

*Receptionist work,* he thought. *That's all it is. Debit this, credit that.
This came in, that went out. Any person sitting at a counter can do this.*

The thought sat heavy. He had left biology -the one subject that had actually
made sense to him, where everything had a logic, where he had felt like he
belonged -for this.

*Partners in nation building.*

It felt like a tagline now. Something said to fill a room with clapping.

---

He reached the bus stand and stopped.

The coaching institute across the road had put up a new banner. Big, bright, a
little faded at the edges from the afternoon sun.

*Congratulations to our AIR Rankers -Foundation & Intermediate.*

Six photographs. Six faces. All smiling the particular smile of people who had
cleared something hard and knew it.

Arjun looked at it for a moment.

These were supposed to be his people. His future. They looked like they had
always known they were in the right room.

He looked away.

And then -without fully knowing why -he thought of Pranav Bhaiya.

Not warmly. More the way you think of the person who recommended something that
didn't work out. Pranav Bhaiya had said CA was the right call. He was a CA
himself -Fortune 500, finance, the whole picture that had made this choice feel
safe two years ago.

Arjun found the contact. He called.

## The Classification Problem
 
**Arjun:** Par Bhaiya -yeh sab toh easy hai na ultimately? Matlab jo aap

**Pranav Bhaiya:** Tu biology ka student hai. Ek cheez poochhta hoon. PAS

**Pranav Bhaiya:** Woh kaam kya karta tha exactly?

## Teaching Flow

### Part A — What the Blood Report Must Assume

*Open Class 1 with this question:*

> *"Arjun ki jo blood report bani thi typhoid mein — usne ek basic assumption li thi. Kya assumption?"*

*(Let students think. Then reveal.)*

**The assumption: the patient is alive and being treated.**

The doctor didn't prepare the report assuming "yeh patient kal nahi rahega, toh aaj hi sab kuch sell karo." He assumed: treatment will continue. Life goes on.

That is **Going Concern**.

When a company prepares accounts, it assumes: *we will continue to operate indefinitely.* Not shutting down tomorrow. Our assets are not being sold off in panic.

This one assumption changes everything:
- Machinery valued at cost minus depreciation — not fire-sale price
- Long-term contracts recorded as ongoing — not immediately settled
- Future expenses planned for — not ignored

> *"Agar doctor yeh assume kare ki patient kal nahi rahega — toh woh typhoid treat karna chhod dega. Isi tarah, agar company going concern assume na kare — toh balance sheet fire-sale value pe hogi. Koi sahi decision nahi le paayega."*

---

**Money Measurement — What the Blood Report Can and Cannot Show**

A blood report shows what can be *measured*. Haemoglobin: 11.2 g/dL. WBC count: 8,000. These are numbers.

The blood report does NOT show: *"This patient has extraordinary willpower."* Real. Affects recovery. But not on the report.

Accounting is the same. Only what can be expressed in **money** is recorded.

A factory worth ₹50 crore — on the books. A brilliant team that took 10 years to build — not on the books. A brand that people trust — only if purchased, not if self-built.

**Limitation:** The most valuable things in a company — culture, brand loyalty, employee quality — often don't appear at all. Accounting always shows an incomplete picture.

> *"Tere blood report mein teri courage nahi aati. Lekin teri courage teri recovery mein sab se badi cheez thi. Yahi limitation hai money measurement ki."*

---

### Part B — The Matching Step (Building on Byju's)

Students already know from Chapter 0 that Byju's booked ₹90,000 in Year 1 for a 3-year course. That was the revenue error.

Now ask: *"Par sirf revenue ka sawaal nahi tha. Expenses ka kya?"*

If Byju's earned ₹30,000 of revenue in Year 1 (what SHOULD have been recognised), then ONLY the expenses of DELIVERING Year 1 should appear in Year 1's P&L. Teachers' salaries for Year 1 content, platform costs for Year 1, support costs for Year 1 — only these go in Year 1.

Year 2 delivery costs go in Year 2. Year 3 in Year 3.

**This is Matching Concept:**

> Revenue ko usi period ke expenses se match karo jisme woh revenue actually earn hua.

Period Profit = Period Revenue − **Matched** Expenses

Not all expenses. Only those that belong to this period's revenue.

---

### Part C — Three Conventions

**Consistency**

Arjun's father runs a small business. In Year 1 he valued closing stock using FIFO. In Year 2 he switched to Weighted Average because it showed lower profit (lower tax). In Year 3, back to FIFO because the business looked better.

*Question for class: "Is this allowed? What's the problem?"*

The blood report changes format every year. Year 1 not comparable with Year 2. Investors, banks, the government — all compare last year vs this year. If the method changes every year, comparison is meaningless.

**Consistency:** Once you pick a method, stick with it year on year. Change only for a valid reason — and when you do, disclose the change and its impact clearly.

---

**Conservatism / Prudence**

The doctor reading Arjun's typhoid report had a choice:
- "Haemoglobin 11.2 — thoda low hai, par within range. Let's say it's fine."
- "11.2 is borderline. Let's treat it as low and add iron supplements. Better safe than sorry."

The prudent doctor chose the second. He *anticipated* the problem.

**Conservatism in accounting:**
- **Anticipate all losses** — even if not yet confirmed. Provide for bad debts. Write down inventory if net realisable value falls.
- **Do not anticipate profits** — record revenue only when actually earned.

> *"Agar doctor ne Arjun ka haemoglobin 13 maana jab tha nahi — aur treatment nahi di — patient deteriorate karta. Isi tarah, agar accountant possible losses ignore kare ya future profits pehle book kare — balance sheet jhooth bolegi."*

---

**Materiality**

A blood report has 20+ parameters. The doctor focuses on the ones that matter for this patient's condition.

He doesn't spend 10 minutes on uric acid when the patient has typhoid. He focuses on the Widal test, platelet count, haemoglobin — because those are **material** to this diagnosis.

Materiality in accounting:
- A ₹50 pen — expense it immediately, don't capitalise it.
- A ₹50 crore machinery — full accounting, depreciation schedule, everything.
- The ₹50 pen is not material to the financial statements. The machinery is.

Information is **material** if its omission or misstatement could influence the economic decisions of users.

---

### Part D — Accounting Policies and Valuation Bases

*(Foundation Ch 1, Units 5 & 6)*

**Accounting Policies** are the specific choices a company makes about HOW to apply accounting principles.

Within the rules, flexibility exists. For inventory: FIFO or Weighted Average? For depreciation: Straight Line or Written Down Value?

These choices are **accounting policies**. Three things guide the selection:
1. **Prudence** — when in doubt, choose the more conservative option
2. **Substance over Form** — look at economic reality, not legal form
3. **Materiality** — if it doesn't matter much, simplify

A policy, once chosen, should only change if required by law/AS, or if the new policy gives a more accurate picture.

---

**Valuation Bases — Four Ways to Put a Number on Things**

A company has a machine. What is it "worth" in the accounts? Four possible answers:

| Basis | Meaning | Common Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical Cost** | What you originally paid | Fixed assets — most common |
| **Current Cost** | What it would cost to buy the same thing today | Replacement planning |
| **Realisable Value** | What you'd get selling it now in an orderly sale | Inventories (lower of cost or NRV) |
| **Present Value** | Discounted value of future cash flows it generates | Investments, loans |

Traditional accounting mostly uses **historical cost**. The machine stays on books at what you paid minus depreciation — even if market value has doubled.

> *"Jaise teri typhoid journey ka permanent record teri body mein stored hai — scar tissues, strengthened immunity — par blood report mein sirf number dikhta hai. Historical cost bhi waise hi kaam karta hai — past ka recorded truth."*

---

### Part E — Why We Need Accounting Standards

*(Foundation Ch 1, Unit 7 — Introduction)*

Arjun asks sitting at his desk:

*"Main ne samajh liya — kaunsi cheez asset hai, kaunsi liability. Mujhe pata hai accrual kya hota hai, matching kya hai. Lekin — kya doosri company ka accountant bhi wahi samjha tha? Kya dono ki balance sheet compare ho sakti hai?"*

This is the exact question that gave birth to **Accounting Standards**.

*Imagine:*

Two pharmacies. Same city. Same type of business. Same year.

Pharmacy A values drug inventory at FIFO. Pharmacy B uses Weighted Average. Pharmacy A depreciates equipment at 10% SLM. Pharmacy B at 20% WDV. Pharmacy A recognises revenue when goods are dispatched. Pharmacy B only when payment is received.

At year end: Pharmacy A shows profit of ₹15 lakhs. Pharmacy B shows ₹8 lakhs.

Same business. Same market. Seven lakh difference.

A bank wanting to loan to one of them — it cannot compare. An investor looking to buy one — it cannot decide.

**This chaos is exactly what Accounting Standards solve.**

ASs are written policy documents covering:
- **Recognition** — when to record a transaction
- **Measurement** — at what value to record it
- **Presentation** — how to show it in financial statements
- **Disclosure** — what additional information must be revealed

In India, the **Accounting Standards Board (ASB)** under ICAI has been issuing these since 1977.

> *"Jaise pathology labs ka ek standard protocol hota hai — Widal test ke liye exact procedure, haemoglobin measurement ke liye exact method — taaki ek lab ki report doosri lab ki report se compare ho sake. Yahi kaam Accounting Standards karte hain companies ke liye."*

## Teaching Flow

### Part A — GAAP: The Grammar of the Language

From Chapter 0: *"Accounting is the language of business."*

Every language has grammar. Hindi has its rules. English has its rules. You can't mix them and call it a sentence.

**GAAP — Generally Accepted Accounting Principles** — is the grammar of the accounting language.

GAAP is not one document. It is the full system of:
- Authoritative standards (issued by regulatory bodies)
- Commonly accepted practices (developed over decades of use)

In India → **AS and Ind AS** are our GAAP.
Globally → **IFRS** (International Financial Reporting Standards) is the closest thing to a global GAAP.

> *"Jaise English mein subject-verb-object ka ek pattern hai — toh duniya bhar ke log ek doosre ki baatein samajh sakte hain — waise GAAP ek company ki accounts ko readable banata hai kisi bhi trained accountant ke liye."*

---

### Part B — How India's Accounting Standards Get Made

*(Teach this as a story, not a list.)*

Someone notices a problem. Companies are accounting for the same transaction in five different ways. No uniformity. Comparison impossible.

The **Accounting Standards Board (ASB)** — a committee under ICAI, formed in 1977 — takes this up.

The journey of one standard:

1. **Problem identified** → ASB picks the topic
2. **Study group formed** → Deep research, discussion with industry
3. **Preliminary draft** → Written, circulated internally
4. **Draft sent to external bodies** → SEBI, MCA, CBDT, C&AG, SCOPE, CII, FICCI, ASSOCHAM — all give comments
5. **Exposure Draft (ED) published** → Anyone in India can comment
6. **Comments received, draft revised**
7. **Council of ICAI approves**
8. **Accounting Standard issued**

Key point: ASB is independent. ICAI Council cannot modify ASB's draft without consulting ASB. This ensures integrity.

> *"Jaise ek nayi medicine approve hone mein saalon lagte hain — clinical trials, regulatory review, public comment — waise ek accounting standard bhi saalon ki mehnat se aata hai. Yeh kisi ek insaan ka opinion nahi hota."*

### Part C — The Germany Problem: Why India's Rules Aren't Enough

*(Back to Arjun's Chacha.)*

India has AS. They are good standards. But they were written for Indian conditions — Indian laws, Indian business practices, Indian tax rules.

Germany, USA, UK, Japan — each country has developed its own GAAP over decades. Different histories, different legal systems, different business cultures.

By 2000, the problem was acute:
- A company listed on both Bombay Stock Exchange and NYSE had to prepare TWO full sets of accounts. Expensive. Confusing.
- A global investor comparing Indian companies with European companies was comparing accounts written in different dialects. Mistakes inevitable.
- Cross-border loans required financial statements both lenders and borrowers could trust.

The world needed **one accounting language**.

Enter: **IASB — International Accounting Standards Board**, based in London.

IASB issues **IFRS — International Financial Reporting Standards**. Today, 140+ countries use IFRS or have converged toward it. The EU mandated IFRS for all listed companies in 2005. This was the turning point.

---

### Part D — India's Choice: Adopt or Converge?

*(The most important intellectual point of Class 2.)*

By 2006, India needed to move toward IFRS. Global capital flows required it. Two choices:

**Option 1: Adopt IFRS**
Replace Indian AS completely with IFRS, word for word.

*Problem:*
- IFRS is written for developed economies. Assumes freely floating interest rates, deep capital markets, sophisticated instruments — conditions that may not exist in India.
- Certain IFRS requirements would conflict with Indian law.
- Some IFRS standards assume economic conditions that simply don't apply here.

**Option 2: Converge with IFRS**
Take IFRS as the base. Adapt for Indian conditions. Keep it as close as possible, but make specific departures where absolutely necessary.

India chose **Option 2 — Convergence**.

*Result: Ind AS — Indian Accounting Standards.*

**The difference:**
- Ind AS is NOT IFRS. But it is very, very close.
- Where Ind AS departs from IFRS: **Carve-outs** (India removed something from IFRS) or **Carve-ins** (India added something IFRS doesn't have).

> *"Agar IFRS ek internationally used language hai — toh Ind AS us language ka Indian accent hai. Grammar same hai, pronunciation similar hai, kuch words different hain. Par ek English speaker IFRS padh ke Ind AS samajh sakta hai. Yeh bridge hai."*

---

### Part E — Carve-outs: Where India Said "Not Exactly"

*(Illustrative examples — not exhaustive)*

**Example:**
IFRS 9 on financial instruments requires certain investments to be measured at "fair value through profit or loss" — meaning if the value changes, that change immediately hits your P&L.

India felt this would cause too much volatility in reported profits given the nature of Indian capital markets. Ind AS modified this — carve-out.

**The principle:**
India is not rejecting global standards. India is saying: *"We agree with the spirit, but the specific implementation needs to fit our legal and economic reality."* This is responsible convergence, not resistance.

---

### Part F — The Ind AS Roadmap

*(Who is already on Ind AS — and when did they switch?)*

India implemented Ind AS in phases:

- **Phase 1 (2016–17):** Listed companies + unlisted companies with Net Worth ≥ ₹500 crore
- **Phase 2 (2017–18):** Companies with Net Worth ≥ ₹250 crore; companies listed on SME exchanges

Today: All listed companies and unlisted companies with Net Worth ≥ ₹250 crore must follow **Ind AS**. Others follow the older **AS** framework. Non-corporate entities — their own track.

*Three sets of standards. Three types of entities. As a CA, you need to know which applies where.*

## Teaching Flow

### Part A — The Framework: Rules for the Blood Report Itself

*(Inter Ch 2 — Framework for Preparation and Presentation of Financial Statements)*

We now know what standards are and who makes them. But there's a deeper question:

*What should a good financial statement look like? What qualities should it have?*

ICAI's ASB issued the **Framework for Preparation and Presentation of Financial Statements** in July 2000. This is not a standard for one specific transaction. It is the *philosophy* behind all standards.

Think of it as the medical ethics code. Before a doctor reads a specific blood report, underlying principles exist: the report should be accurate, legible, use standard units, include reference ranges. These aren't specific to typhoid or malaria — they apply to ALL reports.

The Framework is the same for financial statements.

---

### Part B — The Four Qualities of a Good Blood Report

*(Qualitative Characteristics — Inter Ch 2, Section 5)*

A blood report is useful only if it has four qualities:

**1. Understandability**

The report must be readable by a reasonably informed, reasonably diligent person. Not a rocket scientist, not a 5-year-old.

In accounting: financial statements must be understandable by a person with reasonable accounting knowledge. No hiding information in 200 pages of footnotes that no one reads.

**2. Relevance**

For typhoid — Widal test and platelet count are relevant. Uric acid is not.

In accounting: information is relevant if it can influence an economic decision. Relevance is connected to **materiality** — if omitting or misstating something would affect a decision, it is material and must be disclosed.

**3. Reliability**

The haemoglobin reading should be the actual haemoglobin — not approximated, not biased.

In accounting, reliable information is:
- **Faithfully represented** — shows what it claims to show
- **Substance over Form** — captures economic reality, not just legal form
- **Neutral** — no bias toward making the company look better or worse
- **Prudent** — conservative where uncertainty exists
- **Complete** — no important omissions

> *"Byju's ki accounts mein quality kahan missing thi? Reliability. Substance over form fail hua — advance received tha, par income dikhayi. Neutral nahi tha — accounts bana rahe the taaki valuation bade, investors aayein."*

**4. Comparability**

The same patient's blood report, taken three months apart, must use the same units and reference ranges. Otherwise comparison is impossible.

In accounting: FS must be comparable — across periods (same company, different years) and across entities (different companies, same year). This is why Consistency and Accounting Standards matter so deeply.

### Part C — The Elements, Precisely Defined

*(Inter Ch 2, Section 6 — Building on Chapter 0's body analogy)*

In Chapter 0, Pranav Bhaiya explained assets and liabilities through the body. Now we formalise those intuitions:

**Asset:**
> *A resource controlled by the enterprise as a result of past events, and from which future economic benefits are expected to flow.*

Three parts:
1. Controlled by the enterprise (not necessarily owned — a leased machine is still an asset if you control it)
2. Result of past events (something that already happened)
3. Future economic benefits expected (it will generate cash, reduce future cost, or otherwise bring value)

*Chapter 0 link: "Company ki haddiyan, muscles, glycogen — controlled, from past purchase, generating future output."*

**Liability:**
> *A present obligation of the enterprise arising from past events, the settlement of which is expected to result in an outflow of resources.*

Three parts:
1. Present obligation (legal or constructive — you are currently obligated)
2. From past events (obligation already created)
3. Settlement = outflow of resources

*Chapter 0 link: "Advance received from customer = liability. Past event: customer paid. Present obligation: deliver the course. Settlement: deliver or refund."*

**Equity:**
> *The residual interest in the assets of the enterprise after deducting all its liabilities.*

Equity = Assets − Liabilities. Not a separate thing — what remains after everything else.

*Chapter 0 link: "Soul power. Company ki apni net worth."*

**Income:**
> *Increases in economic benefits during the accounting period — in the form of inflows or enhancements of assets, or decreases of liabilities — that result in increases in equity.*

Includes both Revenue (from ordinary operations) and Gains (from peripheral activities).

**Expense:**
> *Decreases in economic benefits during the accounting period — in the form of outflows or depletions of assets, or incurrences of liabilities — that result in decreases in equity.*

Includes both operating costs and losses.

---

**Recognition Criteria:**

An element is recognised in financial statements when:
1. **Probable** — future economic benefits will flow (in or out)
2. **Reliably Measurable** — value can be measured with sufficient reliability

> *"Ek doctor report mein sirf those findings likhta hai jo actual test se confirmed hain — probable and measurable. Woh nahi likha ki 'maybe patient has some other condition too.' Usi tarah accounts mein sirf woh record hota hai jo probable hai aur reliably measure kiya ja sake."*

### Part D — Capital and Capital Maintenance

*(Inter Ch 2, Section 9)*

This concept answers: *What is profit, really?*

**Financial Capital Maintenance** (more common):
Profit exists only if the financial amount of net assets at the end is more than at the beginning — after adjusting for distributions to owners and contributions from owners.

Simple version: You started with ₹100 crore. You end with ₹120 crore. After accounting for any dividends paid and money put in by owners — the remaining ₹20 crore increase is profit.

**Physical Capital Maintenance:**
Profit exists only if the physical productive capacity of the enterprise is maintained.

Meaning: If all your machines are running at the same capacity as before, AND you have surplus — that surplus is profit. If you used your apparent "profit" just to replace worn-out machinery, you haven't profited — you've maintained what you had.

> *"Jaise ek insaan kehta hai 'mera wajan 70 kg tha, abhi bhi 70 kg hai — main healthy hoon.' Par agar usne 5 kg muscle khoya aur 5 kg fat gain kiya — number same hai, par capacity badal gayi. Financial capital sirf number dekhta hai. Physical capital dekhta hai ki actual productive capacity kaisi hai."*

### Part E — Applicability: Who Follows Which Standard

*(Inter Ch 3 — the Class 2 cliffhanger gets answered)*

**The Three-Question Test**

Before applying any Accounting Standard to any specific situation:

1. **Does this AS apply to this enterprise?**
   - AS applies to any enterprise engaged in commercial, industrial, or business activities.
   - Pure charitable activity — AS does NOT apply.
   - But if even a small part of activity is commercial — AS applies to ALL activities.

2. **Does this AS apply to these financial statements?**
   - Some standards apply only to consolidated statements.
   - Some apply to both standalone and consolidated.
   - Check the standard's scope.

3. **Does this AS apply to this specific item?**
   - Some standards have exceptions for certain items.
   - Check the standard's specific exclusions.

---

**For Corporate Entities — The Companies Act Connection**

| Companies Act 2013 Section | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Section 129(1)** | Every company must prepare FS in accordance with AS notified under Section 133 |
| **Section 133** | Central Government prescribes standards of accounting — done through Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules, 2021 |
| **Section 143(3)(e)** | Auditor MUST report whether, in his opinion, the FS comply with applicable accounting standards |

*What happens if accounts don't comply?*
- The company must disclose: what the deviation is, why they deviated, what the financial impact is.
- The auditor must mention this in the audit report.
- This is not optional — disclosure is legally required.

---

**Three Sets of Standards — Who Uses What**

| Entity Type | Standard Used |
|---|---|
| Listed companies + Unlisted companies with Net Worth ≥ ₹250 crore | **Ind AS** |
| Companies not covered above (smaller unlisted) | **AS** — Companies (AS) Rules, 2021 |
| Non-corporate entities (partnership firms, trusts, societies, individuals) | **AS issued by ICAI** |

---

**The Mandatory Status for Non-Corporate Entities**

For a partnership firm, there is no law requiring AS compliance. The Partnership Act doesn't mandate it.

But: if a CA is auditing that firm, the CA is still bound by ICAI's mandatory standards.

This means:
- If the firm's accounts don't follow AS, the CA cannot ignore it.
- The CA must disclose the deviation in the audit report.
- The CA cannot certify the accounts are "true and fair" if they are not following applicable standards.

**Responsibility split:**
- Preparing FS in compliance = Management's responsibility.
- Reporting whether FS comply = Auditor's responsibility.
- Both are accountable.

> *"Lab technician ki zimmedari — sahi report banana. Doctor ki zimmedari — report sahi hai, tab hi patient ko do. CA ek hi insaan hai jo dono role nibhata hai. Report banata bhi hai, certify bhi karta hai. Yahi baat CA ko itna significant banati hai."*
