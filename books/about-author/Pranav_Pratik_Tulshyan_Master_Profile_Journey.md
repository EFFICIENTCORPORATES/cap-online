## 3. CA Journey — Foundation (formerly CPT) (Common Proficiency Test)
 
### Context & Decision
- Began CA preparation right after Class 12, enrolling in a CA coaching institute in Kathmandu.
- The institute was a local and newly opened institute (**just 2 years old**) — not a national-level institute. Teachers were not national-level faculty.

### The Trigger to Target AIR 1
Two things happened together that made him aim for All India Rank 1 in Foundation (formerly CPT):
 
1. **The Teacher's Philosophy:** The owner/teacher of his Kathmandu institute had a practice of making students raise their hands in class — publicly declaring whether they were targeting a "pass" or targeting a "rank." The teacher's belief was: *"If you are giving six months to something, why not dream of being the best?"* He pushed every student to at least aim for a rank, so their name would be recognised at a national level.
2. **Foundation (formerly CPT) was 100% Objective (MCQ):** Foundation (formerly CPT) at that time was a 200-mark objective examination. For someone who had always feared descriptive writing, this was a revelation. No marks could be deducted for handwriting or presentation. Either you get the marks or you don't. This removed his single biggest fear and became one of his strongest motivating factors.
### Raising His Hand — The Public Declaration
When the teacher asked the class who was targeting a rank, Pranav raised his hand. This made him visible to everyone in the institute. Some peers mocked him — whispering, *"Look at him, sitting in Kathmandu, thinks he'll get All India Rank."* He would hear this on recess, in corridors.
 
His response, both internally and to others, was a philosophy he held firmly:
> *"I am not guaranteeing the result. I am guaranteeing the work."*
 
He reasoned: shame only comes if you declared a goal and then didn't do the work. He was doing the work. So there was nothing to be ashamed of. The results are never in anyone's hands — only the effort is. He stayed loyal to that truth.
 
### The PCO Booth Call
To validate that AIR 1 was actually achievable by a normal student, he did something very interesting. ICAI releases the phone numbers of the top 3 rankers after each attempt. He obtained the number of a top-3 ranker from the June 2014 Foundation (formerly CPT) attempt and walked to a PCO booth (internet calling was not easily available).
 
- He tried for 3–4 days. The first and second rankers did not pick up or someone else answered.
- Finally, he reached the **3rd ranker** — an approximately 80-second call.
- What struck him: the person's voice was completely normal. Not exceptional, not intimidating. A regular student.

>Till now he always had an internal beleif that , the All India First Rankers are someone Out of this world. They are born and raised differently. These people start their All India Rank Jounry from their childhood and are spcial people , and in no way ordinary student like him.  But that call, changed this perception for always, and in a good way..!   

- When asked what he studied: *"Institute materials and ICAI materials. I practised."*
- When asked how many hours: *"Normal. 3–4 hours after institute."*
This was exactly what Pranav was already doing. He realised that the gap between him and a ranker was not talent — it might be the study materials. He immediately went to his teacher and asked for the books of that specific institute (a prominent South India–based institute whose students were regularly ranking).
 
His teacher tried for a week but could not procure those books. Instead, the teacher offered the books from his institute's library — books from 6–7 different authors, all past papers, all previous years' questions. The teacher said: *"Finish all of these first, then we will arrange the other books you need."*
 
Pranav accepted this and made a plan: complete every author's book, every past year paper, every question in the library. **Practise, Practise, Practise was the key strategy.**
 
### The Subconscious Doubt
Throughout preparation, he publicly declared to his teachers at his institue that AIR 1 was his target. But deep down, privately, a part of him doubted whether it was truly possible. He never showed that doubt to anyone. He kept studying. He kept declaring.
 
After the exams (single-day format — two 2-hour sessions), he felt confident overall but was not fully happy — a couple of questions had come from outside the syllabus, and he had some uncertainty about those.
 
### Foundation (formerly CPT) Result
- **Date of Result:** 19 January 2015
- **Score:** 188/200 = **94%**  [*One of the Lowest marks for AIR 1*]
- **Rank:** **All India Rank 1**
- A friend from the institute called him with the news.
The moment he found out was not just a result — it was a life-defining realisation. Every motivational quote he had heard and half-believed — about commitment, about possibility, about the power of focused effort — became real and personal in that instant. The subconscious doubt dissolved permanently.
 
> *"The rank was not the achievement. The feeling — and the confidence it gave me for a lifetime — that was the achievement."*
 
### Historic Significance
This was the **first time in the 70+ year history of ICAI** that a student from outside India (a foreign student) had ever achieved All India Rank 1 in any ICAI examination. It had never happened before.

## 4. CA Journey — Intermediate (formerly IPCC) (Integrated Professional Competence Course)
 
### Context After Foundation (formerly CPT) Result
- Overnight, Pranav became a public figure within the CA fraternity of Nepal. Media coverage, newspaper articles, photographs. A genuine celebrity in the CA community.
- Some people attributed his Foundation (formerly CPT) AIR 1 to luck — "it's MCQ-based, maybe he guessed correctly." This was an external narrative he had to live with.
- The result was also bittersweet in terms of pressure: now everyone was watching. Every move was calculated. He was invited to give talks to Foundation (formerly CPT) students at his institute. His every public statement became scrutinised.
### Decision: Stay in Kathmandu or Move to Delhi
Intermediate (formerly IPCC) was a completely different examination — fully descriptive, 7 subjects (5 of which were entirely new to him), and nationally competitive. Delhi's Laxmi Nagar was considered the prime market for CA coaching. Many people advised him to move to laxmiNagar for Exam Preparations.
 
He chose to **stay with his Kathmandu teacher.** His reasoning: the biggest value his teacher had given him was not syllabus understanding — it was the change in mindset and the direction of thinking. That was worth more than any national-level faculty.
 
He also publicly declared, in a national television interview in Nepal, that he was targeting **All India Rank 1 in Intermediate (formerly IPCC)** as well.
 
### The New Challenges
1. **Descriptive examinations:** He now had to write long-form answers. His handwriting had actually deteriorated during Foundation (formerly CPT) preparation (no writing practice for 6–7 months). Speed, clarity, and presentation were now critical.
2. **Time constraint:** Results came in January 2015. Intermediate (formerly IPCC) exams were in November 2015. Only ~10 months of preparation time.
3. **The weight of expectations:** Unlike Foundation (formerly CPT) where only his institute knew him, now the entire Nepali CA fraternity was watching.
### Key Strategy Shift — Handwriting as Training
From Day 1 of Intermediate (formerly IPCC) preparation, he made a deliberate decision: **make all notes by hand.** Never print, never type. Every question solved in class, every summary, every homework — handwritten. The logic was simple: handwriting speed and quality can only improve through consistent daily practice over months. You cannot improve it 10 days before exams. So the entire 8-month preparation period became a daily handwriting and writing speed training programme.
 
### Reaching Out to Rankers Again
He repeated the strategy from Foundation (formerly CPT) — finding and speaking with Intermediate (formerly IPCC) rankers.
- He identified the few people in Kathmandu who had gotten Intermediate (formerly IPCC) ranks in recent years (a very small number — most rankers came from India).
- He spoke to one woman who had achieved a single-digit All India Rank in Intermediate (formerly IPCC). She motivated him significantly.
- He also called the **2nd ranker of the May 2015 Intermediate (formerly IPCC) attempt** and discussed preparation strategy.
### The Single Most Important Strategy — The Summary System
From one of these interactions (a person who had achieved AIR 9 in Foundation (formerly CPT)), he learned what became his most impactful technique:
 
**For every practical question:**
- Solve the question fully the first time (in class or as homework)
- Immediately after solving, write a short summary: What was given? What was asked? What are the key pointers to remember for this question?
- In 1st revisions , Practice solving the questions and add in the summary any Mistakes or concepts you missed. Make your summary even better. 
- In subsequent revisions (2nd, 3rd revision), read only the summary, not the full question (Only 5-10% questiosn to be solved in 3rd revision)
- Ensure that on the day before each exam, you can go through the summary of every question in that subject
The underlying logic: ICAI exams have a 1-day gap between papers. You must be able to revise the entire subject in those 24 hours. With a vast syllabus, the only way to do this is through compressed, self-written summaries. He firmly believed:
> *"What you do not revise the day before the exam, you cannot write in the exam."*
 
He also had a strong belief in self-written notes over faculty notes or printed materials — because writing is understanding, and because writing was also his practice medium (For Speed & Neatness).
 
### The Nepal Earthquake — April 2015
In April 2015 (25th April 2015 to be precise), during a Cost Accounting class (the Overhead chapter was being taught, it was approximately 11:56 AM), a massive earthquake struck Kathmandu. The classroom began to shake. Students evacuated. Outside, the scene was devastating — roads cracking, buildings collapsing, poles down, compound walls crumbling.
 
- This was the **2015 Nepal Earthquake** — one of the deadliest in Nepal's history, with the epicentre only 50–70 kilometres from Kathmandu.
- Thousands of people died across Nepal.
- By the grace of God, all four members of his family — who were in four different locations at the time — were safe.
- Institutes and schools were closed for 15–20 days.
- A second significant tremor followed in May 2015, causing further disruption.
- For safety, people had to stay in open areas, sleeping in tents, unable to go indoors.
- Many people left Kathmandu. 
- In total, approximately **25–30 days of normal study were lost.**
This was not a few lost days — this was 10% of his entire preparation time, gone. And unlike students in Delhi or South India who may have lost 3–5 days at most, he lost nearly a month.
 
### The Mental Reframe — From Rank to Percentage
When normalcy returned and he sat back at his study table, the thought arrived clearly: *"Those 30 days are gone. Other serious students in India did not lose those days. They studied for 30 more days than me. I do not deserve to be AIR 1 anymore."*
 
The goal of AIR 1 began to disturb him psychologically — it left no room for error, and he knew he had already accumulated a structural disadvantage. The 100 Percentile thinking was somewhere disturbing him a lot, so he found out a solution. Shifting from Percentile to Percentage.
 
His solution was a deliberate mental reframe:
 
**He stopped thinking about AIR 1. He started thinking about 85%.**
 
He took the mark sheets of the top 3 rankers from the May 2015 Intermediate (formerly IPCC) attempt and, subject by subject, wrote down what score he personally could realistically achieve. Accounts — his strongest subject — he targeted 90+. Others he targeted 75–80. The total came to approximately **85%.**
 
The logic was mathematical and psychological:
- Historical Intermediate (formerly IPCC) toppers averaged approximately 78–80%.
- If he scored 85%, he would almost certainly rank in the top 1–3 regardless.
- But more importantly, 85% gave him a **15% cushion.** He could make errors. He could have one bad paper. That mental room made him calmer and more focused. The AIR -1 , which meant 100% percentile was disturbing and left no room for errors.
He never abandoned the vision of AIR 1 — it was still written on his study table as it had been since Foundation (formerly CPT). He just gave his brain the cushion of a percentage target so it could function without anxiety.
 
### The Nepal Blockade — October–November 2015
Just weeks before the Intermediate (formerly IPCC) examinations, a new crisis hit Nepal. A political dispute led to a **blockade of the Nepal-India border** by communities in the Terai region. This became known as the Nepal Blockade of 2015.
 
- Thousands of trucks carrying supplies from India were halted.
- Petrol prices skyrocketed from ~₹70–80/litre to ₹500–600/litre on the black market.
- Public transportation was nearly non-existent.
- Fuel was being sold in bottles by the roadside.
His exam centre was approximately 7–10 kilometres from his home. He had no private transport. During the 15-day examination period, getting to the centre and returning each day was a logistical challenge — crowded tempos, long waits, uncertainty.
 
Despite Household and Business related challenges his family faced, The study time at home was largely unaffected. The challenge was purely the commute. But the psychological weight of seeing the city struggle — combined with everything else that year — was significant.
 
### The Audit Paper — The Unexpected Advantage
The November 2015 Intermediate (formerly IPCC) was later recognised as one of the toughest in history. The combined pass percentage for both groups was approximately **4%** — the lowest in Intermediate (formerly IPCC) history at that time.
 
The Audit paper was particularly hard. A compulsory question (carrying 5 marks) appeared from the **Auditing Pronouncements** — a thick, often-overlooked document issued by ICAI that is not part of the standard study material.
 
Pranav had made the decision, during preparation, to go through the entire Auditing Pronouncements book (approximately 300–400 pages), make summaries, and include it in his revision plan. Most students had not done this.
 
He was able to attempt that 5-mark question. Most students left it blank. He later reflected on this as one of the most satisfying moments of his CA journey — not because of 5 marks, but because it validated his philosophy: *leave no stone unturned, and revise everything before the exam.*
 
### Intermediate (formerly IPCC) Result
- **Date of Result:** 1 February 2016
- **Score:** 544/700 = **77.7%**
- **Rank:** **All India Rank 1** (sole AIR 1 — no shared rank, unlike Foundation (formerly CPT))
- **Attempt:** First attempt
The history repeated. The teacher who had motivated him to raise his hand in class, who had himself not fully believed this was possible — was stunned. The students who attributed his Foundation (formerly CPT) Achievment to Luck were silent.

Despite the fact that he had targeted for 85% and acheived far less than the target , still ended up with an AIR 1.  

Post the exams, he went to his maternal home ( in Jamshedpur, Jharkhand ) as part of relaxation and also complete his GMCS & OP Training mandated by ICAI.   

back in nepal, with the ongoing instability in Kathmandu, The family had been packing boxes to permanently move to India . During the results period — his Intermediate (formerly IPCC) result came while the house was being packed.

### His Perspective on Missing AIR 1
He has always been a firm beleiver of Efforts over results. As he used to say in his Foundation (formerly CPT) Times , "*I can guarantee the work, but I can't guarantee the result"* 

A Firm beleiver of the Geeta Shlok:

**कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन।मा कर्मफलहेतुर्भूर्मा ते सङ्गोऽस्त्वकर्मणि॥**

So, A person can never demand the fruits of his hardwork. he always have to be honest with his work. 

Also, regarding the rank Position , He has always maintained that the top 10 rankers are fundamentally the same — in terms of preparation quality and commitment. He said the same thing when he got AIR 1 in Foundation (formerly CPT) and Intermediate (formerly IPCC): *"The AIR 10 is no different from me. Maybe there's a small luck factor involved."*
 
He does have one small, honest regret: ICAI issues a press release with photographs only for the top 3 rankers. Had he been top 3, people would have seen that the double AIR 1 holder from Kathmandu had continued his journey. At AIR 5, there is no press release, no photograph — and from the outside, AIR 5 looks the same as AIR 50 to most people.
 
### The Learning from CA Finals
The CA Finals added a third and final layer to the philosophy that had been building across all three levels:
 
- **Foundation (formerly CPT) learning:** *"If you commit fully, nothing is impossible. The leap of faith is real."*
- **Intermediate (formerly IPCC) learning:** *"When obstacles come, don't change your goals. Change your methods. Be stubborn about the destination, flexible about the path."*
- **Finals learning:** *"Even when you do everything right — the goal, the method, the discipline, the sacrifice — sometimes the result still doesn't match the dream. And that is completely fine. The work was honest. The work was real. The result was never yours to own."*
He returns again to what he said in Foundation (formerly CPT) Class when people used to Mock him for targeting for All India Rank while staying in Kathmandu:
> *"I am not guaranteeing the result. I am guaranteeing the work."*

## 10. Teaching Philosophy & Personal Beliefs
 
### On Students and Learning
- He idolises **Eklavya** from the Mahabharata — the student who, without a formal teacher, mastered archery through sheer self-directed effort and surpassed formally taught students. He believes CA is fundamentally a **self-study course.** A teacher's role is to provide direction, motivation, and clarity — but the effort must come entirely from the student.
- He believes the luckiest students are those who can sit and study without financial pressure or other obligations. He acknowledges he was one of those lucky students — his family ensured he could focus entirely on studies. He saw close friends who were more naturally talented than him but had to divide their time between studies and work, and whose results suffered accordingly.
- He is deeply indebted to the one teacher in Kathmandu who shifted his thinking — not for teaching him content, but for teaching him how to think about goals.
### On Goals and Results
- *"Be stubborn about your goals. Be flexible about your methods."*
- *"I am not guaranteeing the result. I am guaranteeing the work."*
- *"Karmanya Vadhikaraste Ma Phaleshu Kadachana"* — the Bhagavad Gita verse he lives by: you have the right to perform your duty, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions.
### On Success and Ranks
- He has always maintained that rank holders are not fundamentally different from those just below them. The difference between AIR 1 and AIR 5 or AIR 10 is marginal. He held this view even when he was AIR 1 — which is perhaps its most credible form.
### On Teaching Style
- He tends to explain concepts logically — making student truly understand, not just rote learn it.
- He is a naturally curious person who gets excited by new concepts and new areas of learning.
- He is warm, conversational, and talks a lot. Students are likely to experience him as an **elder brother** rather than a formal authority figure.
- He is 29 — close in age to the students he will teach. Approachable, relatable, peer-like.
- He loves to dance, travel, meet new people, and explore new places.
- He seeks out **ambitious people** — not in terms of wealth, but in terms of intention: people who want to learn, grow, and contribute something meaningful.
### Favourite Quote
Steve Jobs — which he first said aloud on the very first day of his Foundation (formerly CPT) class, when his teacher asked every student to give their personal tagline:
> *"The only way to do great work is to love what you do."*
 
He lived this literally. He loved studying. He was genuinely excited by new concepts. He looked forward to opening his books. That love — not discipline alone — is what he believes carried him through two earthquakes, a blockade, a cross-country move, and three levels of ICAI examinations.
