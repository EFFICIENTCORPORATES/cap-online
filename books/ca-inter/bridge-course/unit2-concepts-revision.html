Kahaani — The Story

Context before Concept · Why GAAP exists
Kahaani
5 Accountants. 5 Different Profits. One Problem.

Imagine a single company hands its books to five different accountants and asks each one to compute the profit for the year. All five are qualified. All five are working with the same set of transactions. And yet — when the results come back — all five give a different number. Not slightly different. Significantly different.

One accountant values inventory at FIFO. Another uses weighted average. One depreciates at 10%, the next at 15%. One books revenue the moment an order is confirmed. Another waits for cash to arrive. The result? Five accountants, five profits, and zero trust in any of the numbers.

This is the problem GAAP was born to solve. Generally Accepted Accounting Principles — a set of conventions, rules, and procedures — exist for one reason: so that any accountant, anywhere, working on the same company's books, produces a result that is comparable, consistent, and trustworthy.

Real World — What Happens When You Get It Wrong

In 2022–23, Byju's was the world's most valued edtech company. Billions of dollars from global investors like SoftBank and BlackRock. The outside looked perfect — aggressive growth, global expansion, celebrity endorsements. But when auditors examined the internal "blood report," the financial statements told a completely different story.

Byju's was booking three years of subscription revenue in Year 1 itself — without matching the expenses (server costs, teacher salaries, course delivery) that would be incurred in Years 2 and 3. The Matching Concept and Accrual Concept were both violated. Profits looked astronomical. And when investors finally saw the reality, the company's valuation collapsed, auditors resigned, and global trust evaporated — all because two fundamental accounting concepts were ignored.

This unit gives you those exact concepts. Not theory for theory's sake — the rules that, when broken, collapse billion-dollar companies.

2.1
Introduction — The Need for GAAP

If 5 accountants independently compute profit using their own methods, they will produce 5 different results. GAAP solves this by providing a common framework — so financial statements are comparable, consistent, and trustworthy across all enterprises.

GAAP = a set of conventions, rules, and procedures that define accepted accounting practice at any point in time.

2.2
Accounting Concepts

Fundamental assumptions on which financial statements are prepared. They govern how transactions are recognised and recorded. Broad and pervasive in nature — e.g. Going Concern, Dual Aspect.

2.3
Accounting Principles

A body of doctrines that explain current accounting practices and guide the selection of conventions. A principle must satisfy 5 conditions:

  1. Based on real assumptions (objective)
  2. Simple, understandable, and explanatory
  3. Followed consistently across periods
  4. Able to reflect future predictions
  5. Informational for users making economic decisions
2.4
Accounting Conventions

Customs or practices that have emerged from accounting tradition over time. Need not have universal application. Examples: Conservatism, Consistency, Materiality.

Key distinction: Concepts are theoretical foundations; conventions are practical traditions; principles are the formal rules that govern application.

2.5(a)
Entity Concept

The business is a legally and financially separate entity from its owner. Owner's personal transactions are never recorded in business books. Capital invested by the owner is treated as a liability of the business to the owner. Drawings reduce capital.

Example: Mr. X invests ₹7,00,000 to start a business. His personal car is not an asset of the business.

2.5(b)
Money Measurement Concept

Only transactions that can be expressed in monetary terms are recorded. Non-monetary factors — employee morale, management quality, brand reputation — are excluded from accounts.

Limitation: Land bought at ₹40 crore in 2000 is still shown at ₹40 crore even if current market value is ₹400 crore. Inflation distorts real value over time.

2.5(c)
Periodicity Concept

The economic life of a business is divided into defined accounting periods (typically 1 April – 31 March) rather than waiting until the business ends to compute profit.

This enables: (i) comparison of results across periods; (ii) uniform matching of revenues and expenses; (iii) timely information to stakeholders.

Example: Reliance Industries cannot wait until the business closes to calculate profit. On 31 March, factory operations pause, stock is counted, and the period is closed.

2.5(d)
Accrual Concept

Revenue and expenses are recognised when earned or incurred — not when cash is received or paid. This is also called the Mercantile basis of accounting.

4 timing cases to remember:

  • Cash received before earning revenue → Liability (advance received)
  • Cash received after earning revenue → Trade Receivable (debtor)
  • Cash paid before expense is incurred → Prepaid Expense (asset)
  • Cash paid after expense is incurred → Outstanding Payable (liability)
Exam Tip

In subjective answers, never write "when cash is received or paid." The mandatory keyword is "Mercantile basis" — or the phrase "when earned or incurred." Missing this keyword loses marks even if the rest of the answer is correct.

2.5(e)
Matching Concept

Expenses of a period must be matched with the revenues of that same period. Periodic Profit = Periodic Revenue − Matched Expenses.

Example — Mr. PK Garments: Revenue earned ₹12,00,000 (even though only ₹10,00,000 cash received). Rent for 12 months due but only 11 months paid → 1 month outstanding recognised. Accrual + Matching + Periodicity all work together.

Kaam Ki Baat
Byju's — What Happens When Matching Fails

Byju's sold 3-year course subscriptions and booked the entire fee as revenue in Year 1. But the costs of delivering those courses — server infrastructure, teacher salaries, customer support — would be incurred across all 3 years. Revenue was front-loaded; expenses were deferred. The Matching Concept was violated at scale.

When global auditors (Deloitte) examined the books, they resigned citing inability to verify the accounts. The company's valuation collapsed from $22 billion to near zero. One accounting concept. One violation. Catastrophic outcome.

2.5(f)
Going Concern Concept

The entity is assumed to continue operations indefinitely in the foreseeable future. There is no intention or need to liquidate or curtail operations materially.

Implication: Assets are valued at historical cost (held for future use), not at liquidation value. If the going concern assumption is in doubt, this fact must be specifically disclosed.

This assumption is one of the 3 Fundamental Accounting Assumptions under AS 1.

2.5(g)
Cost Concept (Historical Cost)

Assets are recorded at their original acquisition cost — the objective, verifiable amount actually paid. Not at market value, not at what they are worth today.

Example: Land purchased in 1995 for ₹2,000 is shown in the Balance Sheet at ₹2,000 even if today's market value is ₹1,00,000. The pen you bought at ₹10 is recorded at ₹10 — not at your estimate of what it is worth.

Limitation: Historical cost loses relevance over time due to inflation, making comparisons across different periods misleading.

2.5(h)
Realisation Concept

A change in the value of an asset is recorded only when it is realised (i.e., through an actual transaction). Unrealised gains are not recorded; unrealised losses, however, are provided for under conservatism.

Example: Computers purchased at ₹20,000 are not restated at ₹30,000 current market value. Revenue of ₹20,000 is recognised only when the sale is completed — not when an order is placed.

2.5(i)
Dual Aspect Concept

The core of double-entry bookkeeping. Every transaction has two equal and opposite aspects.

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

There are 8 possible combinations (asset↑ liability↑; asset↑ equity↑; etc.). This equation always balances — it is used to compute profit: Profit = Closing Equity − Opening Equity (adjusted for capital and drawings).

Story Link

Think of Newton's Third Law: every action has an equal and opposite reaction. In accounting, every transaction does the same — one account goes up, another comes down (or two go up, one on each side). The universe stays balanced. So does the Balance Sheet.

2.5(j)
Conservatism (Prudence)

Anticipate no future income. Provide for all possible losses. When alternatives exist, choose the lesser value for assets / the higher value for liabilities.

Golden rule for current assets: "Cost or NRV — whichever is lower."

3 qualitative requirements it ensures: (i) Prudence; (ii) Neutrality; (iii) Faithful Representation.

Common Trap

A tricky T/F question uses the word "materialised" in a statement about Conservatism. Students read "materialised" and immediately think of the Realisation concept — and get it wrong. Stay alert: the question is testing Conservatism. The word "materialised" means "occurred" or "became real" — not Realisation concept.

2.5(k)
Consistency

The same accounting method must be followed consistently from period to period so that results are comparable across years. Applies when alternatives are equally acceptable (e.g., WDV vs SLM depreciation; FIFO vs Weighted Average for inventory).

Change is allowed only when: (a) required to comply with an AS or statute; (b) necessary to show a true and fair view. Effect of any change must be disclosed.

Consistency is also one of the 3 Fundamental Accounting Assumptions under AS 1.

2.5(l)
Materiality

An item is material if its omission or misstatement would likely influence an economic decision. All material items must be disclosed strictly. Insignificant items may be handled practically (e.g., stationery fully expensed in year of purchase rather than depreciated).

Materiality depends on: (i) absolute amount; (ii) size of the business; (iii) nature of the information; (iv) level of the decision-maker.

Materiality is not a separate accounting concept — it is the exception that allows other concepts to be set aside when the effect is trivial.

2.6
Fundamental Accounting Assumptions (AS 1)

Three assumptions are presumed to be followed in all financial statements. If followed, no separate disclosure is required. If any is NOT followed, this fact must be specifically disclosed.

AssumptionWhat it meansIf not followed
Going Concern Entity will continue indefinitely; no plan to liquidate or curtail operations Disclose — assets may be stated at NRV/liquidation value instead of cost
Consistency Same accounting policies applied period to period Disclose — explain change and quantify the effect on financial statements
Accrual Revenues/expenses recognised when earned/incurred, not on cash basis Disclose — cash basis accounting must be specifically stated
2.7
Financial Statements

Structured financial representations of an enterprise's position and performance. Includes:

  • Balance Sheet — financial position (what it owns and owes)
  • Statement of Profit & Loss — performance (profit/loss for the period)
  • Cash Flow Statement — circulation (how cash was generated and used)
  • Notes — supplementary disclosures and accounting policies
2.7.1
Qualitative Characteristics of Financial Statements

11 attributes that make financial information useful to users:

#CharacteristicWhat it means (one line)
1UnderstandabilityReadily understood by users with reasonable knowledge of business and accounting
2RelevanceInfluences economic decisions (has predictive or confirmatory value)
3ReliabilityFree from material error and bias
4ComparabilityPermits comparison across firms and across time periods
5MaterialityOmission or misstatement would influence decisions
6Faithful RepresentationTransactions represented honestly — what they actually are
7Substance over FormEconomic reality governs over strict legal form
8NeutralityFree from bias in selection or presentation of information
9PrudenceDegree of caution under uncertainty — do not overstate assets or income
10CompletenessAll material information included within bounds of cost and materiality
11Full, fair and adequate disclosureNothing significant omitted; presented without distortion
